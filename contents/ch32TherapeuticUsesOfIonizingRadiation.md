* Explain the concept of radiotherapy and list typical doses for cancer therapy.

Therapeutic applications of ionizing radiation, called radiation therapy or **radiotherapy**, have existed since the discovery of X-rays and nuclear
radioactivity. Today, radiotherapy is used almost exclusively for cancer
therapy, where it saves thousands of lives and improves the quality of life and
longevity of many it cannot save. Radiotherapy may be used alone or in
combination with surgery and chemotherapy (drug treatment) depending on the type
of cancer and the response of the patient. A careful examination of all
available data has established that radiotherapy’s beneficial effects far
outweigh its long-term risks.

### Medical Application

The earliest uses of ionizing radiation on humans were mostly harmful, with many
at the level of snake oil as seen in [[Figure 1]](#Figure1). Radium-doped
cosmetics that glowed in the dark were used around the time of World War I. As
recently as the 1950s, radon mine tours were promoted as healthful and
rejuvenating—those who toured were exposed but gained no benefits. Radium salts
were sold as health elixirs for many years. The gruesome death of a wealthy
industrialist, who became psychologically addicted to the brew, alerted the
unsuspecting to the dangers of radium salt elixirs. Most abuses finally ended
after the legislation in the 1950s.

![The image shows an old advertisement of radium material branded as UNDARK with the tagline “Shines in the Dark.”](../resources/Figure_32_03_01.jpg 'The properties of radiation were once touted for far more than its modern use in cancer therapy. Until 1932, radium was advertised for a variety of uses, often with tragic results. (credit: Struthious Bandersnatch.)')
{: #Figure1}

Radiotherapy is effective against cancer because cancer cells reproduce rapidly
and, consequently, are more sensitive to radiation. The central problem in
radiotherapy is to make the dose for cancer cells as high as possible while
limiting the dose for normal cells. The ratio of abnormal cells killed to normal
cells killed is called the **therapeutic ratio**, and all radiotherapy
techniques are designed to enhance this ratio. Radiation can be concentrated in
cancerous tissue by a number of techniques. One of the most prevalent techniques
for well-defined tumors is a geometric technique shown in [[Figure 2]](#Figure2)
. A narrow beam of radiation is passed through the patient from a variety of
directions with a common crossing point in the tumor. This concentrates the dose
in the tumor while spreading it out over a large volume of normal tissue. External treatment commonly uses X-rays produced by a medical linear
accelerator. Gamma-ray sources and particle beams, including protons and carbon
ions, are also used. Different beams deposit energy differently, allowing the
treatment to be planned to deliver a large tumor dose while sparing nearby
healthy tissue. For some particle beams, RBE must also be considered.

![The image shows a man lying on a flat surface. A gamma ray machine is rotated around the man.](../resources/Figure_32_03_02.jpg 'The 60Co source of ( \\gamma );-radiation is rotated around the patient so that the common crossing point is in the tumor, concentrating the dose there. This geometric technique works for well-defined tumors.')
{: #Figure2}

Another form of radiotherapy places sealed radioactive sources inside or close
to a tumor. This is called **brachytherapy**. For example, small seeds containing
iodine-125 or palladium-103 can be implanted to treat prostate cancer. Their
low-energy photons deliver most of their dose near the seeds.

Radiopharmaceuticals are used for cancer therapy when they can be localized well
enough to produce a favorable therapeutic ratio. Thyroid cancer is commonly
treated utilizing radioactive iodine. Thyroid cells concentrate iodine, and
cancerous thyroid cells are more aggressive in doing this. An ingenious use of
radiopharmaceuticals in cancer therapy tags antibodies with radioisotopes.
Antibodies produced by a patient to combat his cancer are extracted, cultured,
loaded with a radioisotope, and then returned to the patient. The antibodies are
concentrated almost entirely in the tissue they developed to fight, thus
localizing the radiation in abnormal tissue. The therapeutic ratio can be quite
high for short-range radiation. There is, however, a significant dose for organs
that eliminate radiopharmaceuticals from the body, such as the liver, kidneys,
and bladder. As with most radiotherapy, the technique is limited by the
tolerable amount of damage to the normal tissue.

Radiotherapy is prescribed using **absorbed dose in grays (Gy)**, rather than
effective dose in sieverts. Treatments are often divided into **fractions**:
separate sessions that allow healthy tissue time to recover. A conventional
course may deliver about 2 Gy per session over several weeks. Other techniques
use fewer, larger fractions. [[Table 1]](#Table1) gives illustrative examples;
the actual dose depends on the tumor and surrounding tissue.

Radiotherapy can cure some cancers, including selected early-stage lung
cancers. It can also be combined with other treatments or used to relieve pain
and other symptoms when a cure is not possible. Both tumor control and damage
to healthy tissue must be considered.

Finally, it is interesting to note that chemotherapy employs drugs that
interfere with cell division and is, thus, also effective against cancer. It
also has almost the same side effects, such as nausea and hair loss, and risks,
such as the inducement of another cancer.

### Section Summary

- Radiotherapy is the use of ionizing radiation to treat ailments, now limited
  to cancer therapy.
- The sensitivity of cancer cells to radiation enhances the ratio of cancer
  cells killed to normal cells killed, which is called the therapeutic ratio.
- Doses for various organs are limited by the tolerance of normal tissue for
  radiation. Treatment is localized in one region of the body and spread out in
  time.

### Problems &amp; Exercises

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

A beam of 168-MeV nitrogen nuclei is used for cancer therapy. If this beam is directed onto a 0.200-kg tumor and gives it an absorbed dose of 2.00 Gy, how many nitrogen nuclei were stopped? Assume each nucleus deposits its full kinetic energy in the tumor.

:::

::: solution

The absorbed energy is $$E=Dm=(2.00\text{ J/kg})(0.200\text{ kg})=0.400\text{ J}$$. Each stopped nucleus deposits $$168\text{ MeV}=2.69\times10^{-11}\text{ J}$$, so

$$N=\frac{0.400\text{ J}}{2.69\times10^{-11}\text{ J}}=1.49\times10^{10}\text{ nuclei}.$$

RBE would be relevant to comparing biological effects with a reference beam, but is not needed to calculate absorbed energy.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

(a) If the average molar mass of compounds in food is 50.0 g/mol, how many molecules are there in 1.00 kg of food? (b) How many ion pairs are created in 1.00 kg of food, if it is given an absorbed dose of 1000 Gy and it takes 32.0 eV to create an ion pair? (c) Find the ratio of ion pairs to molecules. (d) If these ion pairs recombine into a distribution of 2000 new compounds, how many parts per billion is each?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

(a) Find the number of moles in 1.00 kg, then multiply by Avogadro's number. (b) Convert the dose to energy deposited, then divide by the energy per ion pair. (c) Divide the results from (b) by (a). (d) Divide the total ion pairs by 2000 to find molecules per new compound type, then find the ratio to total molecules.

**Solution**

(a) Number of molecules in 1.00 kg of food:

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{moles} = \frac{1000 \text{ g}}{50.0 \text{ g/mol}} = 20.0 \text{ mol} $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ N_{\text{molecules}} = (20.0 \text{ mol})(6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ molecules/mol}) = 1.20 \times 10^{25} \text{ molecules} $$
</div>

(b) Number of ion pairs created:

First, find energy deposited. Since $$1 \text{ Gy} = 1 \text{ J/kg}$$:

<div class="equation">
$$ E = (1000 \text{ Gy})(1.00 \text{ kg}) = 1000 \text{ J} $$
</div>

Convert to eV:

<div class="equation">
$$ E = 1000 \text{ J} \times \frac{1 \text{ eV}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}} = 6.24 \times 10^{21} \text{ eV} $$
</div>

Number of ion pairs:

<div class="equation">
$$ N_{\text{ion pairs}} = \frac{6.24 \times 10^{21} \text{ eV}}{32.0 \text{ eV/ion pair}} = 1.95 \times 10^{20} \text{ ion pairs} $$
</div>

(c) Ratio of ion pairs to molecules:

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{Ratio} = \frac{1.95 \times 10^{20}}{1.20 \times 10^{25}} = 1.63 \times 10^{-5} = \frac{1}{61,500} $$
</div>

This means about 1 molecule in every 61,500 is ionized.

(d) Parts per billion for each new compound:

If $$1.95 \times 10^{20}$$ ion pairs recombine into 2000 different new compounds:

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{Molecules per compound} = \frac{1.95 \times 10^{20}}{2000} = 9.75 \times 10^{16} \text{ molecules} $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{Parts per billion} = \frac{9.75 \times 10^{16}}{1.20 \times 10^{25}} \times 10^{9} = 8.1 \times 10^{0} \approx 8 \text{ ppb} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The 1000-Gy dose is 1 kGy, within the food-treatment range of about 0.1–10 kGy (100–10,000 Gy). The small ion-pair fraction follows from the large number of molecules. The 8-ppb result assumes equal distribution among 2000 products; concentrations alone do not establish their biological effects. Food irradiation doses are expressed as absorbed dose in Gy.
:::

</div>

::: problem

(a) A cancer patient is exposed to  $$\gamma  $$
 rays from a 5000-Ci  $${}^{60}\text{Co} $$
 transillumination unit for 32.0 s. The  $$\gamma  $$
 rays are collimated in such a manner that only 1.00% of them strike the patient. Of those, 20.0% are absorbed in a tumor having a mass of 1.50 kg. What is the absorbed dose in Gy to the tumor, if the total gamma-ray energy per decay is taken to be 1.25 MeV for this simplified calculation? None of the  $$\beta  $$
 s from the decay reach the patient. (b) Is the dose consistent with stated therapeutic doses?

:::

::: solution

**Solution**

**(a)** The activity is $$R=(5000\text{ Ci})(3.70\times10^{10}\text{ s}^{-1}/\text{Ci})=1.85\times10^{14}\text{ s}^{-1}$$. With the stipulated energy per decay,

$$E_{\text{absorbed}}=Rt(0.0100)(0.200)(1.25\text{ MeV})(1.602\times10^{-13}\text{ J/MeV})=2.37\text{ J}.$$

The absorbed dose is $$D=E/m=2.37\text{ J}/1.50\text{ kg}=1.58\text{ Gy}$$.

**(b)** This is of the same order as a conventional 2-Gy fraction in Table 1, rather than a complete 60-Gy course. Clinical suitability cannot be judged from tumor dose alone; fractionation and normal-tissue doses also matter.

**Discussion**

The calculation uses the question's simplified total of 1.25 MeV per decay. Actual cobalt-60 emits two principal gamma rays per decay, about 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, totaling about 2.50 MeV. Using that total with the same geometry and absorption assumptions would double the calculated dose.

:::

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

Large amounts of  $${}^{65}\text{Zn} $$
 are produced in copper exposed to accelerator beams. While machining contaminated copper, a physicist ingests  $$50.0 \mu Ci $$
 of  $${}^{65}\text{Zn} $$ .
 Each  $${}^{65}\text{Zn} $$
 decay emits an average  $$\gamma  $$
-ray energy of 0.550 MeV, 40.0% of which is absorbed in the scientist's 75.0-kg body. What dose in mSv is caused by this in one day?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Calculate the number of decays in one day from the activity, multiply by the energy per decay and the absorption fraction to get total energy deposited, convert to joules, and divide by body mass to get dose in Gy. Since gamma rays have radiation weighting factor w_R = 1, the dose in Sv equals the dose in Gy, then convert to mSv.

**Solution**

Convert activity to Bq:

<div class="equation">
$$ R = 50.0 \text{ μCi} = 50.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ Ci} \times \frac{3.70 \times 10^{10} \text{ Bq}}{1 \text{ Ci}} = 1.85 \times 10^{6} \text{ Bq} $$
</div>

Number of decays in one day:

<div class="equation">
$$ N_{\text{decays}} = (1.85 \times 10^{6} \text{ s}^{-1})(86,400 \text{ s}) = 1.60 \times 10^{11} \text{ decays} $$
</div>

Energy absorbed (40.0% of emitted energy):

<div class="equation">
$$ E = (1.60 \times 10^{11})(0.550 \text{ MeV})(0.400) = 3.52 \times 10^{10} \text{ MeV} $$
</div>

Convert to joules:

<div class="equation">
$$ E = (3.52 \times 10^{10} \text{ MeV})(1.602 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J/MeV}) = 5.64 \times 10^{-3} \text{ J} $$
</div>

Dose in Gy:

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{Dose in Gy} = \frac{5.64 \times 10^{-3} \text{ J}}{75.0 \text{ kg}} = 7.52 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Gy} $$
</div>

Since w_R = 1 for gamma rays:

<div class="equation">
$$ \text{Dose in Sv} = 7.52 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Sv} = 0.0752 \text{ mSv} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The dose of 0.075 mSv in one day is relatively small but not negligible. To put this in perspective, the average annual background radiation dose is about 3 mSv, so this one-day dose from ingested $${}^{65}\text{Zn}$$ represents about 2.5% of the annual background dose. However, this calculation only accounts for one day's exposure. The actual total dose depends on how long the zinc-65 remains in the body. With a radiological half-life of 244 days, $${}^{65}\text{Zn}$$ decays relatively slowly, but biological elimination removes zinc from the body with a biological half-life of about 300-500 days. The effective half-life (combining radioactive decay and biological elimination) is approximately 140 days. Over this longer period, the cumulative dose would be significantly higher—roughly 150 times the one-day dose, or about 11 mSv total. This cumulative dose is still well below occupational limits (20-50 mSv/y) but represents a serious contamination incident that would require medical follow-up and investigation. This problem underscores the importance of proper safety protocols when machining or handling materials that have been exposed to radiation. Even if the copper appears "cold" (low external radiation), internal contamination from ingested or inhaled particulates can deliver significant doses. The 40% absorption fraction is realistic for gamma rays from an internally distributed source, as some gammas escape the body without interacting.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

(a) Background radiation due to  $${}^{226}\text{Ra} $$
 averages only 0.01 mSv/y, but it can range upward depending on where a person lives. Find the mass of  $${}^{226}\text{Ra} $$
 in the 80.0-kg body of a man who receives a dose of 2.50-mSv/y from it, noting that each  $${}^{226}\text{Ra} $$
 decay emits a 4.80-MeV  $$\alpha  $$
 particle. You may neglect dose due to daughters and assume a constant amount, evenly distributed due to balanced ingestion and bodily elimination. (b) Is it surprising that such a small mass could cause a measurable radiation dose? Explain.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use the alpha-particle radiation weighting factor $$w_R=20$$ to convert the stated equivalent dose to absorbed energy. Then infer activity and mass.

**Solution**

**(a)** The annual absorbed dose is $$D=H/w_R=(2.50\text{ mSv})/20=1.25\times10^{-4}\text{ Gy}$$. In 80.0 kg this is 0.0100 J per year. Each decay deposits $$4.80\text{ MeV}=7.69\times10^{-13}\text{ J}$$, giving $$1.30\times10^{10}$$ decays per year, or 412 Bq.

With $$t_{1/2}=1600\text{ y}$$,

$$N=\frac{Rt_{1/2}}{\ln2}=3.00\times10^{13},\qquad m=\frac{N}{N_A}(226\text{ g/mol})=1.13\times10^{-8}\text{ g}=11.3\text{ ng}.$$

**(b)** A small mass still contains many radioactive atoms, each depositing MeV-scale energy. The large radiation weighting factor also contributes to the equivalent dose.

**Discussion**

This uses the stipulated uniform distribution and complete absorption, while neglecting daughter nuclides. Real radium distributions and dose assessments require more detailed models. The radiation weighting factor used here is a protection convention, not a universal RBE for every biological effect.

:::

::: problem

The annual radiation dose from  $${}^{14}\text{C} $$
 in our bodies is 0.01 mSv/y. Each  $${}^{14}\text{C} $$
 decay emits a  $${\beta }^{-} $$
 averaging 0.0750 MeV. Taking the fraction of  $${}^{14}\text{C} $$
 to be $$N({}^{14}\text{C})/N({}^{12}\text{C})=1.3\times10^{-12}$$ ,
 and assuming the body is 13% carbon, estimate the fraction of the decay energy absorbed. (The rest escapes, exposing those close to you.)

:::

::: solution

For 1.00 kg of body mass, the carbon mass is 130 g. Hence

$$N_{14}=\frac{130\text{ g}}{12\text{ g/mol}}N_A(1.3\times10^{-12})=8.5\times10^{12}.$$

Using $$t_{1/2}=5730\text{ y}$$ gives an activity of 32.5 Bq/kg. The emitted energy per kilogram per year is

$$E_{\text{emitted}}=(32.5\text{ s}^{-1})(3.156\times10^7\text{ s})(0.0750\text{ MeV})(1.602\times10^{-13}\text{ J/MeV})=1.23\times10^{-5}\text{ J}.$$

For beta particles, $$w_R=1$$, so the stated equivalent dose corresponds to $$D=1.0\times10^{-5}\text{ Gy/y}$$. The absorbed fraction is therefore $$f=(1.0\times10^{-5})/(1.23\times10^{-5})\approx0.81$$, or **81%**.

:::

::: problem

For an illustrative population model, take Australia's population to be 26.0 million and a baseline cancer mortality of 110 deaths per 100,000 people per year. Suppose everyone receives an extra 0.05 mSv each year. After allowing for latency, estimate the annual increase in cancer deaths using the stipulated model coefficient of 0.0200 deaths per person-Sv of annual exposure. What percentage of the baseline mortality is this? Treat these population and risk inputs as assumptions for the exercise.
:::

::: solution

The annual collective dose is

$$(26.0\times10^6)(0.05\times10^{-3}\text{ Sv})=1300\text{ person-Sv}.$$

The stipulated model gives $$1300(0.0200)=26$$ additional deaths per year after latency. The assumed baseline is $$(26.0\times10^6)(110/100000)=28600$$ deaths per year, so the relative increase is

$$\frac{26}{28600}\times100\%=0.091\%.$$

This is a model estimate using the supplied coefficients and comparison rate. Such a small predicted increment does not constitute an observable count attributable to the exposure.
