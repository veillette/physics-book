**Food irradiation** treats food with ionizing radiation to reduce harmful
microorganisms, control insects, and delay spoilage. Approved processes use
gamma rays, X-rays, or electron beams. Their energies and operating conditions
are controlled so that the treated food does not become radioactive.

Gamma-ray sources include cobalt-60, which emits photons with an average energy
of about 1.25 MeV. A typical plant passes packaged food through a radiation field
for a controlled time (see [[Figure 1]](#Figure1)). When not in use, the source
is shielded, often in a deep pool of water.

![A conveyor carries food past a shielded radiation source.](../resources/Figure_32_04_01.jpg 'In a food-irradiation plant, shielding protects workers while food receives a controlled dose. A gamma-ray source can be lowered into a deep pool of water when not in use.')
{: #Figure1}

Radiation damages microorganisms directly and by producing reactive fragments,
including **free radicals**—atoms or molecules with an unpaired electron. These
fragments can damage DNA and other essential cell components. The required
absorbed dose depends on the food and the organisms being controlled. Food
treatment doses can be thousands of grays, much higher than doses tolerated by
a person. Eating the food does not expose the consumer to that radiation dose.

Food irradiation has been extensively evaluated. The FDA, WHO, and other public
health authorities support its safety under approved conditions. Irradiation
can cause some nutrient and flavor changes, as can cooking and other
preservation methods; the effects depend on the food and dose. These changes
are considered when a process is approved.

Irradiation does not replace sanitation, refrigeration, or proper cooking.
Treated food can be contaminated again and must still be handled safely.

### Section Summary

- Food irradiation uses controlled doses of gamma rays, X-rays, or electrons
  to reduce microorganisms and pests and to delay spoilage.
- Approved processes do not make food radioactive and have been evaluated for
  safety. Proper food handling is still required.
- Free radicals have an unpaired electron and can damage microbial cell
  components. Treatment effects depend on the food, organism, and absorbed dose.

### Conceptual Questions

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Does food irradiation leave the food radioactive? To what extent is the food altered chemically for low and high doses in food irradiation?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Distinguish ionization and chemical changes from activation of nuclei.

**Solution**

Approved food-irradiation methods and energies do not make the food radioactive. They do create some ions and reactive molecules, changing a fraction of the food’s chemistry. Lower treatment doses generally cause smaller changes; higher doses can produce more changes in vitamins, flavor, color, or texture, depending on the food and conditions.

**Discussion**

The radiation source does not mix with the food. This statement concerns regulated treatment conditions, not every conceivable nuclear irradiation; see the [CDC food-irradiation explanation](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-health/food-irradiation/index.html).

**Answer**

Proper food irradiation does not leave food radioactive; chemical changes depend on dose, food, and treatment conditions.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Compare a low dose of radiation to a human with a low dose of radiation used in food treatment.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use absorbed-dose units consistently and compare their scales.

**Solution**

Food treatment commonly uses absorbed doses on the scale of $0.1$–$10\text{ kGy}$, or $100$–$10\,000\text{ J/kg}$. Even a “low” dose in that context can greatly exceed doses described as low for human radiation protection. For example, $0.1\text{ kGy}=100\text{ Gy}$, whereas a uniform $0.1\text{ Gy}$ photon exposure is a thousand times smaller.

**Discussion**

Food is treated to suppress organisms, not to preserve living human tissue. Sieverts describe weighted biological exposure and should not be substituted for grays in food-treatment specifications; see the [FDA food-irradiation overview](https://www.fda.gov/food/irradiation-food-packaging/overview-irradiation-food-and-packaging).

**Answer**

“Low dose” is context-dependent; a low food-treatment dose can be enormously greater than a low human exposure.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Suppose one food irradiation plant uses a  $${}^{137}\text{Cs} $$
 source while another uses an equal activity of  $${}^{60}\text{Co} $$ .
 Assuming equal fractions of the  $$\gamma  $$
 rays from the sources are absorbed, why is more time needed to get the same dose using the  $${}^{137}\text{Cs} $$
 source?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Compare emitted gamma energy per decay at equal activity.

**Solution**

Cobalt-60 typically emits two gamma photons, about $1.17$ and $1.33\text{ MeV}$, totaling $2.50\text{ MeV}$ per decay. Cesium-137’s daughter produces an approximately $0.662\text{ MeV}$ gamma in roughly $85\%$ of parent decays, giving about $0.56\text{ MeV}$ per decay on average. Equal activities therefore give different gamma powers.

**Discussion**

For equal energy-absorption fractions and treated masses, dose rate follows emitted gamma power. The cesium source takes roughly $2.50/0.56\approx4.4$ times as long in this idealization.

**Answer**

Cesium-137 supplies less gamma energy per parent decay, so equal activity delivers the same absorbed dose more slowly.

:::

food irradiation
