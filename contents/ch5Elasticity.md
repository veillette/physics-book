We now move from consideration of forces that affect the motion of an object (
such as friction and drag) to those that affect an object’s shape. If a
bulldozer pushes a car into a wall, the car will not move but it will noticeably
change shape. A change in shape due to the application of a force is a
**deformation**. Even very small forces are known to cause some deformation. For
small deformations, two important characteristics are observed. First, the
object returns to its original shape when the force is removed—that is, the
deformation is elastic for small deformations. Second, the size of the
deformation is proportional to the force—that is, for small deformations,
Hooke’s law is obeyed. In equation form, **Hooke’s law** is given by

<div class="equation">
 $$ F=k\Delta L, $$
</div>

where $$ \Delta L $$ is the amount of deformation (the change in length, for
example) produced by the force $$ F $$, and $$ k $$ is a proportionality
constant that depends on the shape and composition of the object and the
direction of the force. Note that this force is a function of the deformation $$ \Delta L $$ —it is not constant as a kinetic friction force is. Rearranging this
to

<div class="equation">
 $$ \Delta L=\frac{F}{k} $$
</div>

makes it clear that the deformation is proportional to the applied
force. [Figure 1](#Figure1) shows the Hooke’s law relationship between the
extension $$ \Delta L $$ of a spring or of a human bone. For metals or springs,
the straight line region in which Hooke’s law pertains is much larger. Bones are
brittle and the elastic region is small and the fracture abrupt. Eventually a
large enough stress to the material will cause it to break or fracture.
**Tensile strength** is the greatest tensile stress a material can withstand.
Permanent deformation can begin at a smaller stress, called the **yield strength**,
before the material breaks.

The proportionality constant $$ k $$ depends upon a number of factors for the
material. For example, a guitar string made of nylon stretches when it is
tightened, and the elongation $$ \Delta L $$ is proportional to the force
applied (at least for small deformations). Thicker nylon strings and ones made
of steel stretch less for the same applied force, implying they have a larger $$ k $$
(see [Figure 2](#Figure2)). Finally, all three strings return to their normal
lengths when the force is removed, provided the deformation is small. Most
materials will behave in this manner if the deformation is less than about 0.1%
or about 1 part in $$ 10^{3} $$.

We now consider three specific types of deformations: changes in length (tension
and compression), sideways shear (
stress), and changes in volume. All deformations are assumed to be small unless
otherwise stated.

### Changes in Length—Tension and Compression: Elastic Modulus

A change in length $$ \Delta L $$ is produced when a force is applied to a wire
or rod parallel to its length $$ L_{0} $$, either stretching it (a tension) or
compressing it. (
See [Figure 3](#Figure3).)

![Figure a is a cylindrical rod standing on its end with a height of L sub zero. Two vectors labeled F extend away from each end. A dotted outline indicates that the rod is stretched by a length of delta L. Figure b is a similar rod of identical height L sub zero, but two vectors labeled F exert a force toward the ends of the rod. A dotted line indicates that the rod is compressed by a length of delta L.](/resources/Figure_05_03_03.svg '(a) Tension. The rod is stretched a length \( \Delta L \) when a force is applied parallel to its length. (b) Compression. The same rod is compressed by forces with the same magnitude in the opposite direction. For very small deformations and uniform materials, \( \Delta L \) is approximately the same for the same magnitude of tension or compression. For larger deformations, the cross-sectional area changes as the rod is compressed or stretched.')
{: #Figure3}

Experiments have shown that the change in length ( $$ \Delta L $$)
depends on only a few variables. As already noted, $$ \Delta L $$ is
proportional to the force $$ F $$ and depends on the substance from which the
object is made. Additionally, the change in length is proportional to the
original length $$ L_{0} $$ and inversely proportional to the cross-sectional
area of the wire or rod. For example, a long guitar string will stretch more
than a short one, and a thick string will stretch less than a thin one. We can
combine all these factors into one equation for $$ \Delta L $$:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \Delta L=\frac{1}{Y}\frac{F}{A} L_{0}, $$
</div>

where $$ \Delta L $$ is the change in length, $$ F $$ the applied force, $$ Y $$
is a factor, called the elastic modulus or Young’s modulus, that depends on the
substance, $$ A $$ is the cross-sectional area, and $$ L_{0} $$ is the original
length. [Table 1](#Table1) lists values of $$ Y$$ for several materials—those
with a large $$ Y $$ are said to have a large tensile stiffness because they
deform less for a given tension or compression.

<div class="title">
The Stretch of a Long Cable
</div>

Suspension cables are used to carry gondolas at ski resorts. (See [Figure 4](#Figure4)) Consider a suspension cable that includes an unsupported span of 3020 m. Calculate the amount of stretch in the steel cable. Assume that the cable has a diameter of 5.6 cm and the maximum tension it can withstand is $$ 3.0\times 10^{6}\N $$.

![Ski gondolas travel along suspension cables. A vast forest and snowy mountain peaks can be seen in the background.](../resources/Figure_05_03_04.jpg 'Gondolas travel along suspension cables at the Gala Yuzawa ski resort in Japan. (credit: Rudy Herman, Flickr)')
{: #Figure4}

**Strategy**

The force is equal to the maximum tension, or $$ F=3.0\times 10^{6}\N $$. The
cross-sectional area is $$ \pi r^{2}=2.46 \times 10^{-3} \mm $$. The equation $$ \Delta L=\frac{1}{Y}\frac{F}{A} L_{0} $$ can be used to find the change in
length.

**Solution**

All quantities are known. Thus,

<div class="equation">
$$ \begin{array}{lll}
\Delta L&=& \left(\frac{1}{210 \times 10^{9}\text{N/m}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{3.0\times 10^{6}\N }{2.46\times 10^{-3} \mm}\right)\left(3020 \m \right)\\
\Delta L&=& 18 \m .
\end{array} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

This is quite a stretch, but only about 0.6% of the unsupported length. Effects
of temperature upon length might be important in these environments.

::::

Bones, on the whole, do not fracture due to tension or compression. Rather they
generally fracture due to sideways impact or bending, resulting in the bone
shearing or snapping. The behavior of bones under tension and compression is
important because it determines the load the bones can carry. Bones are
classified as weight-bearing structures such as columns in buildings and trees.
Weight-bearing structures have special features; columns in building have
steel-reinforcing rods while trees and bones are fibrous. The bones in different
parts of the body serve different structural functions and are prone to
different stresses. Thus the bone in the top of the femur is arranged in thin
sheets separated by marrow while in other places the bones can be cylindrical
and filled with marrow or just solid. Overweight people have a tendency toward
bone damage due to sustained compressions in bone joints and tendons.

Another biological example of Hooke’s law occurs in tendons. Functionally, the
tendon (the tissue connecting muscle to bone) must stretch easily at first when
a force is applied, but offer a much greater restoring force for a greater
strain. [Figure 5](#Figure5) shows a stress-strain relationship for a human tendon.
Some tendons have a high collagen content so there is relatively little strain,
or length change; others, like support tendons (as in the leg) can change length
up to 10%. Note that this stress-strain curve is nonlinear, since the slope of
the line changes in different regions. In the first part of the stretch called
the toe region, the fibers in the tendon begin to align in the direction of the
stress—this is called _uncrimping_. In the linear region, the fibrils will be
stretched, and in the failure region individual fibers begin to break. A simple
model of this relationship can be illustrated by springs in parallel: different
springs are activated at different lengths of stretch. Examples of this are
given in the problems at end of this chapter. Ligaments (tissue connecting bone
to bone)
behave in a similar way.

![The strain on mammalian tendon is shown by a graph, with strain along the x axis and tensile stress along the y axis. The stress strain curve obtained has three regions, namely, toe region at the bottom, linear region between, and failure region at the top.](../resources/Figure_05_03_05.svg 'Typical stress-strain curve for mammalian tendon. Three regions are shown: (1) toe region (2) linear region, and (3) failure region.')
{: #Figure5}

Unlike bones and tendons, which need to be strong as well as elastic, the
arteries and lungs need to be very stretchable. The elastic properties of the
arteries are essential for blood flow. The pressure in the arteries increases
and arterial walls stretch when the blood is pumped out of the heart. When the
aortic valve shuts, the pressure in the arteries drops and the arterial walls
relax to maintain the blood flow. When you feel your pulse, you are feeling
exactly this—the elastic behavior of the arteries as the blood gushes through
with each pump of the heart. If the arteries were rigid, you would not feel a
pulse. The heart is also an organ with special elastic properties. The lungs
expand with muscular effort when we breathe in but relax freely and elastically
when we breathe out. Our skins are particularly elastic, especially for the
young. The elasticity of all organs reduces with age. Gradual physiological
aging through reduction in elasticity starts in the early 20s.

Calculating Deformation: How Much Does Your Leg Shorten When You Stand on It?
</div>

Calculate the change in length of the upper leg bone (the femur) when a 70.0 kg
man supports 62.0 kg of his mass on it, assuming the bone to be equivalent to a
uniform rod that is 40.0 cm long and 2.00 cm in radius.

**Strategy**

The force is equal to the weight supported, or

<div class="equation">
 $$ F=mg =\left(62.0 \kg \right)\left(9.80 \mss \right)=607.6 \N , $$
</div>

and the cross-sectional area is $$ \pi r^{2}=1.257 \times 10^{-3} \mm $$. The equation $$ \Delta L=\frac{1}{Y}\frac{F}{A} L_{0} $$
can be used to find the change in length.

**Solution**

All quantities except $$ \Delta L $$ are known. Note that the compression value
for Young’s modulus for bone must be used here. Thus,

<div class="equation">
$$ \begin{array}{lll}
\Delta L&=& \left(\frac{1}{9\times 10^{9}\text{N/m}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{607.6 \N }{1.257\times 10^{-3} \mm}\right)\left(0.400 \m \right)\\
\Delta L&=& 2\times 10^{-5}\m .
\end{array} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

This small change in length seems reasonable, consistent with our experience
that bones are rigid. In fact, even the rather large forces encountered during
strenuous physical activity do not compress or bend bones by large amounts.
Although bone is rigid compared with fat or muscle, several of the substances
listed in [Table 1](#Table1) have larger values of Young’s modulus $$ Y $$. In
other words, they are more rigid.

::::

The equation for change in length is traditionally rearranged and written in the
following form:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \frac{F}{A}=Y\frac{ \Delta L}{ L_{0}}. $$
</div>

The ratio of force to area, $$ \frac{F}{A} $$, is defined as **stress** (
measured in $$ \text{N/m}^{2} $$ ), and the ratio of the change in length to
length, $$ \frac{ \Delta L}{ L_{0}} $$, is defined as
**strain**
(a unitless quantity). In other words,

<div class="equation">
 $$ \text{stress}=Y \times \text{strain}. $$
</div>

In this form, the equation is analogous to Hooke’s law, with stress analogous to
force and strain analogous to deformation. If we again rearrange this equation
to the form

<div class="equation">
 $$ F= Y A \frac{ \Delta L}{ L_{0}}, $$
</div>

we see that it is the same as Hooke’s law with a proportionality constant

<div class="equation">
 $$ k=\frac{ Y A}{ L_{0}}. $$
</div>

This general idea—that force and the deformation it causes are proportional for
small deformations—applies to changes in length, sideways shear, and changes
in volume.

### Sideways Stress: Shear Modulus

[Figure 6](#Figure6) illustrates what is meant by a sideways stress or a _shearing
force_. Here the deformation is called $$ \Delta x $$ and it is perpendicular to
$$ L_{0} $$, rather than parallel as with tension and compression. Shear
deformation behaves similarly to tension and compression and can be described
with similar equations. The expression for
**shear deformation** is

<div class="equation">
 $$ \Delta x=\frac{1}{S}\frac{F}{A} L_{0}, $$
</div>

where $$ S $$ is the shear modulus (
see [Table 1](#Table1)) and $$ F $$ is the force applied perpendicular to $$ L_{0} $$ and parallel to the cross-sectional area $$ A $$. Again, to keep the
object from accelerating, there are actually two equal and opposite forces $$ F $$

applied across opposite faces, as illustrated in [Figure 6](#Figure6). The
equation shows that, for the same material and applied force, a taller block
undergoes a greater sideways displacement than a shorter one with the same
cross-sectional area. A larger area or a larger shear modulus reduces this
deformation.

Examination of the shear moduli in [Table 1](#Table1)
reveals some telling patterns. For example, shear moduli are less than Young’s
moduli for most materials; for a uniform material the shear modulus is always
less than Young’s modulus. Bone follows this pattern: its shear modulus is only
about one-fifth of its Young’s modulus, which is one reason bones usually break
by shearing or bending rather than by simple tension or compression.

The spinal column (consisting of 26 vertebral segments separated by discs)
provides the main support for the head and upper part of the body. The spinal
column has normal curvature for stability, but this curvature can be increased,
leading to increased shearing forces on the lower vertebrae. Discs are better at
withstanding compressional forces than shear forces. Because the spine is not
vertical, the weight of the upper body exerts some of both. Pregnant women and
people that are overweight (with large abdomens) need to move their shoulders
back to maintain balance, thereby increasing the curvature in their spine and so
increasing the shear component of the stress. An increased angle due to more
curvature increases the shear forces along the plane. These higher shear forces
increase the risk of back injury through ruptured discs. The lumbosacral disc (
the wedge shaped disc below the last vertebrae) is particularly at risk because
of its location.

The shear moduli for concrete and brick are too variable to be listed in the
table, and their shear strength (the stress at which they fail in shear) is
low. Concrete used in buildings can withstand compression, as in pillars and
arches, but is very poor against shear, as might be encountered
in heavily loaded floors or during earthquakes. Modern structures were made
possible by the use of steel and steel-reinforced concrete. Almost by
definition, liquids and gases have shear moduli near zero, because they flow in
response to shearing forces.

Find the mass of the picture hanging from a steel nail as shown in [Figure 7](#Figure7),

**Strategy**

We model the nail as if it deformed purely in shear; a real nail loaded at its

$$ F $$ on the nail (neglecting the nail’s own weight) is the weight

can be solved for $$ F $$.

**Solution**

Solving the equation $$ \Delta x=\frac{1}{S}\frac{F}{A} L_{0} $$ for $$ F $$, we

<div class="equation">

$$ S=80 \times 10^{9} \text{N/m}^{2} $$. The radius $$ r $$

</div>

This is a fairly massive picture, and it is impressive that the nail flexes only

### Changes in Volume: Bulk Modulus

An object will be compressed in all directions if inward forces are applied
evenly on all its surfaces as in [Figure 8](#Figure8). It is relatively easy to
compress gases and extremely difficult to compress liquids and solids. For
example, air in a wine bottle is compressed when it is corked. But if you try
corking a brim-full bottle, you cannot compress the wine—some must be removed if
the cork is to be inserted. The reason for these different compressibilities is
that atoms and molecules are separated by large empty spaces in gases but packed
close together in liquids and solids. To compress a gas, you must force its
atoms and molecules closer together. To compress liquids and solids, you must
actually compress their atoms and molecules, and very strong electromagnetic
forces in them oppose this compression.

![A cube with area of cross section A and volume V zero is compressed by an inward force F acting on all surfaces. The compression causes a change in volume delta V, which is proportional to the force per unit area and its original volume. This change in volume is related to the compressibility of the substance.](/resources/Figure_05_03_09.svg "An inward force on all surfaces compresses this cube. Its change in volume is proportional to the force per unit area and its original volume, and is related to the compressibility of the substance.")
{: #Figure8}

We can describe the compression or volume deformation of an object with an
equation. First, we note that a force “applied evenly” is defined to have the
same stress, or ratio of force to area $$ \frac{F}{A} $$ on all surfaces. The
deformation produced is a decrease in volume. Here $$ \Delta V $$ denotes the
positive amount by which the volume decreases, rather than final volume minus
initial volume. This decrease is found to
behave very similarly to the shear, tension, and compression previously
discussed. (This is not surprising, since a compression of the entire object is
equivalent to compressing each of its three dimensions.) The relationship of the
change in volume to other physical quantities is given by

<div class="equation">
 $$ \Delta V=\frac{1}{B}\frac{F}{A} V_{0}, $$
</div>

where $$ B $$ is the bulk modulus (see [Table 1](#Table1)), $$ V_{0} $$ is the
original volume, and $$ \frac{F}{A} $$ is the force per unit area applied
uniformly inward on all surfaces. Note that no bulk moduli are given for gases.

What are some examples of bulk compression of solids and liquids? One practical
example is the manufacture of industrial-grade diamonds by compressing carbon
with an extremely large force per unit area. The carbon atoms rearrange their
crystalline structure into the more tightly packed pattern of diamonds. In
nature, a similar process occurs deep underground, where extremely large forces
result from the weight of overlying material. Another natural source of large
compressive forces is the pressure created by the weight of water, especially in
deep parts of the oceans. Water exerts an inward force on all surfaces of a
submerged object, and even on the water itself. At great depths, water is
measurably compressed, as the following example illustrates.

:::: example

<div class="title">
Calculating Change in Volume with Deformation: How Much Is Water Compressed at Great Ocean Depths?
</div>

Calculate the fractional decrease in volume ( $$ \frac{ \Delta V}{ V_{0}} $$ )
for seawater at 5.00 km depth, where the force per unit area is $$ 5.00 \times 10^{7} \N/ \mm $$.

**Strategy**

Equation $$ \Delta V=\frac{1}{B}\frac{F}{A} V_{0} $$ is the correct physical
relationship. All quantities in the equation except $$ \frac{ \Delta V}{ V_{0}} $$ are known.

**Solution**

Solving for the unknown $$ \frac{ \Delta V}{ V_{0}} $$ gives

<div class="equation">
 $$ \frac{ \Delta V}{ V_{0}}=\frac{1}{B}\frac{F}{A}. $$
</div>

Substituting known values with the value for the bulk modulus
$$ B $$ from [Table 1](#Table1),

<div class="equation">
$$ \begin{array}{lll}
\frac{ \Delta V}{ V_0}&=& \frac{5.00\times 10^{7} \N/\mm }{2.2\times 10^{9} \N/\mm} \\
\frac{ \Delta V}{ V_0}&=& 0.023=2.3%.
\end{array} $$

</div>

**Discussion**

Although measurable, this is not a significant decrease in volume considering
that the force per unit area is about 500 atmospheres (1 million pounds per
square foot). Liquids and solids are extraordinarily difficult to compress.

::::

Conversely, very large forces are created by liquids and solids when they try to
expand but are constrained from doing so—which is equivalent to compressing them
to less than their normal volume. This often occurs when a contained material
warms up, since most materials expand when their temperature increases. If the
materials are tightly constrained, they deform or break their container. Another
very common example occurs when water freezes. Water, unlike most materials,
expands when it freezes, and it can easily fracture a boulder, rupture a
biological cell, or crack an engine block that gets in its way.

Other types of deformations, such as torsion or twisting, behave analogously to
the tension, shear, and bulk deformations considered here.

### Section Summary

- Hooke’s law is given by
  <div class="equation">
  $$ F=k\Delta L, $$
  </div>

where $$ \Delta L $$ is the amount of deformation (the change in length), $$ F $$ is the applied force, and $$ k $$ is a proportionality constant that depends
on the shape and composition of the object and the direction of the force. The
relationship between the deformation and the applied force can also be written
as

  <div class="equation">
  $$ \Delta L=\frac{1}{Y}\frac{F}{A} L_{0}, $$
  </div>

where $$ Y $$ is _Young’s modulus_, which depends on the substance, $$ A $$ is
the cross-sectional area, and $$ L_{0} $$ is the original length.

- The ratio of force to area, $$ \frac{F}{A} $$, is defined as *stress*,
  measured in $$\text{N/m}^2$$.
- The ratio of the change in length to length, $$ \frac{ \Delta L}{ L_{0}} $$,
  is defined as _strain_ (a unitless quantity). In other words,
  <div class="equation">
    $$ \text{stress}=Y \times \text{strain}. $$
  </div>

- The expression for shear deformation is
  <div class="equation">
    $$ \Delta x=\frac{1}{S}\frac{F}{A} L_{0}, $$
  </div>

where $$ S $$ is the shear modulus and $$ F $$ is the force applied
perpendicular to $$ L_{0} $$ and parallel to the cross-sectional area $$ A $$.

- The decrease in volume, written as a positive amount $$ \Delta V $$, is given
  by
  <div class="equation">
    $$ \Delta V=\frac{1}{B}\frac{F}{A} V_{0}, $$
  </div>

where $$ B $$ is the bulk modulus, $$ V_{0} $$ is the original volume, and $$ \frac{ F}{A} $$ is the force per unit area applied uniformly inward on all
surfaces.

:::: problem

The elastic properties of the arteries are essential for blood flow. Explain the importance of this in terms of the characteristics of the flow of blood (pulsating or continuous).

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

The heart pumps in pulses, and the arteries are elastic, so they stretch under pressure like a spring. Follow what happens to an artery during the pumping stroke (systole) and during the rest between beats (diastole).

**Solution**

The heart ejects blood only during part of each beat. In a resting adult it ejects about $$ 70 \text{ mL} $$ in about $$ 0.3 \s $$, then rests for about $$ 0.5 \s $$. If the arteries were rigid pipes, blood would surge during each stroke and almost stop between strokes.

Because the large arteries are elastic:

1. **During systole** the pressure rises to about $$ 120 \text{ mm Hg} $$ ($$ 16.0 \text{ kPa} $$). The arteries stretch and store part of the pumped blood, along with elastic potential energy in their walls.
2. **During diastole** the heart's valve closes, and the stretched walls spring back. They keep pushing blood forward, so the pressure falls only to about $$ 80 \text{ mm Hg} $$ ($$ 10.7 \text{ kPa} $$) rather than to zero.

The elastic arteries therefore turn the heart's jerky, pulsating output into a much smoother, nearly continuous flow through the small vessels and capillaries. This reduces the peak pressure and the stress on vessel walls and lowers the work the heart must do. When arteries stiffen with age (arteriosclerosis), this smoothing is lost, and the systolic pressure rises.

The elasticity of the arteries is essential because the arteries stretch to store blood and energy during each heartbeat (at about $$ 16.0 \text{ kPa} $$) and spring back between beats, keeping the pressure near $$ 10.7 \text{ kPa} $$ and turning the heart's pulsating output into a nearly continuous flow of blood.

:::: problem

What are you feeling when you feel your pulse? Measure your pulse rate for 10 s and for 1 min. Is there a factor of 6 difference?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

Identify what physically moves under your fingertip, then compare two ways of finding the beats per minute, including the effect of counting uncertainty.

**Solution**

The pulse you feel is a **pressure wave** traveling along the elastic wall of the artery. Each heartbeat sends a surge of blood into the aorta. The artery wall bulges, and the bulge travels outward along the arteries at a few meters per second, much faster than the blood itself flows. Your fingertip feels the artery wall expanding as each wave passes.

Suppose you count 12 beats in $$ 10 \s $$. The rate is

<div class="equation">
$$ \frac{12 \text{ beats}}{10 \s} \times \frac{60 \s}{1 \text{ min}} = 72 \text{ beats/min} $$

One reason is that you can only count whole beats, so a 10-second count is uncertain by about $$ \pm 1 \text{ beat} $$, which becomes $$ \pm 6 \text{ beats/min} $$ ($$ \pm 8\% $$) when multiplied by 6. The same $$ \pm 1 $$ beat in a 60-second count is only $$ \pm 1 \text{ beat/min} $$ ($$ \pm 1.4\% $$). The heart rate also really changes from moment to moment, with breathing for example.

You feel the pressure wave that each heartbeat sends along the elastic artery wall, and the 1-minute count is about 6 times the 10-second count (for example, $$ 12 \text{ beats}/10 \s $$ gives $$ 72 \text{ beats/min} $$), but not exactly, because counting whole beats makes the 10-second result uncertain by about $$ \pm 6 \text{ beats/min} $$ and the heart rate itself varies.

:::: problem

Examine different types of shoes, including sports shoes and thongs. In terms of physics, why are the bottom surfaces designed as they are? What differences will dry and wet conditions make for these surfaces?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

A shoe sole must provide static friction for walking, running, and turning, and it must cushion the foot by deforming. Use $$ f_{s,\text{max}} = \mu_{s} N $$ and the elastic properties of rubber to explain the designs.

**Solution**

Take $$ +y $$ up, perpendicular to the ground, and $$ +x $$ horizontally in the direction the runner pushes off. A $$ 70 \kg $$ person standing has $$ N = (70 \kg)(9.80 \mss) = 686 \N $$. The largest horizontal push the ground can supply is $$ \mu_{s} N $$. With rubber on dry concrete ($$ \mu_{s} = 1.0 $$) that is $$ 686 \N $$; on wet concrete ($$ \mu_{s} = 0.7 $$) it falls to $$ 480 \N $$.

- **Soft rubber soles** have a high coefficient of friction and deform to fit the small bumps of the ground, increasing the true contact area.
- **Tread patterns, grooves, and studs** let the sole grip uneven ground. On wet surfaces the grooves channel water out from under the sole so the rubber still touches the ground instead of sliding on a water film. Cleats and spikes dig into grass or a track and push against the ground almost like a step.
- **Sports shoes** have thick, elastic soles that compress on landing. Stretching the stopping time and distance reduces the force on the body, and some of the elastic energy is returned at push-off. Tread patterns are designed for the motion: circular pivot points on basketball shoes, deep lugs on trail shoes.
- **Thongs (flip-flops)** have thin, flat, often smooth soles. On dry ground they grip adequately, but when wet a water film forms under the flat sole, $$ \mu_{s} $$ drops sharply, and they become slippery. They also offer little cushioning.

Shoe soles use soft, high-friction rubber, tread patterns, and elastic cushioning so that static friction can supply large horizontal forces (about $$ 686 \N $$ for a $$ 70 \kg $$ person on dry concrete) and landings are softened, and wet conditions lower the friction (to about $$ 480 \N $$ here), which is why grooves that channel water away matter and smooth flat soles like those of thongs become slippery.

:::: problem

Would you expect your height to be different depending upon the time of day? Why or why not?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

The spine contains intervertebral discs of soft, elastic material that carry the weight of the upper body. Ask how a compressive force over many hours changes their thickness.

Your height does depend on the time of day, because the weight of your upper body (about $$ 400 \N $$) compresses the intervertebral discs during the day and makes you about $$ 1 \text{ to } 2 \text{ cm} $$ shorter by evening, and the discs recover overnight when you lie down.

:::: problem

Why can a squirrel jump from a tree branch to the ground and run away undamaged,
while a human could break a bone in such a fall?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

The question repeats one from Drag Forces. Here, focus on the elastic properties of bone: compare the stress (force per unit area) in the bones of a squirrel and a human, and compare how fast each lands.

**Solution**

**1. Stress scales with size.** A bone breaks when the stress $$ F/A $$ exceeds its strength. Bone has about the same strength in all animals. A human ($$ 70 \kg $$) is about $$ (70 \kg/0.50 \kg)^{1/3} \approx 5 $$ times larger in each dimension than a squirrel ($$ 0.50 \kg $$). The force needed to stop a falling body is proportional to its mass, which grows as $$ L^{3} $$ (a factor of about 140). The cross-sectional area of the bones grows only as $$ L^{2} $$ (a factor of about 27). For the same deceleration, the stress in the human's bones is about

<div class="equation">
$$ \frac{140}{27} \approx 5 $$
</div>

times larger. A squirrel's bones are therefore far from their breaking stress when it lands, while a human's are much closer.

**2. The squirrel lands more slowly.** Its small mass compared with its area gives it a terminal speed of only about $$ 16 \ms $$, compared with about $$ 40 \ms $$ for a human. Spreading its legs and tail, the squirrel is slowed by air resistance, and it bends its legs on landing to stretch out the stopping distance.

A squirrel survives the jump because the stress in its bones on landing is only about one fifth of what a human's bones experience for the same deceleration (mass grows with size cubed, bone area only with size squared) and because air resistance gives it a much lower landing speed, so a human's bones are pushed much closer to their breaking stress.

:::: problem

Explain why pregnant women often suffer from back strain late in their pregnancy.

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

Find how the extra mass in front of the body changes the position of the center of gravity, and what torque the back muscles must then supply about the base of the spine.

**Solution**

Late in pregnancy, several kilograms of baby, uterus, and fluid sit in front of the spine. To keep the center of gravity over the feet, a pregnant woman leans backward. Even so, the extra mass exerts a forward torque about the lower spine that the back muscles must balance.

Take the pivot at the lower spine. Say $$ 10 \kg $$ of added mass has its center about $$ 0.20 \m $$ in front of the spine. Its weight produces a forward (bending) torque of

<div class="equation">
$$ \tau = r_{\perp} w = (0.20 \m)(10 \kg)(9.80 \mss) = 19.6 \N \cdot \m $$
</div>

The back muscles act only about $$ 0.050 \m $$ behind the pivot, so to balance this torque they need an extra force of

<div class="equation">
$$ F = \frac{19.6 \N \cdot \m}{0.050 \m} = 392 \N $$
</div>

That is about four times the extra weight itself. This force, held for hours each day, tires and strains the back muscles. It also adds to the compression of the intervertebral discs. In addition, pregnancy hormones loosen ligaments, so the muscles carry more of the load.

Pregnant women often suffer back strain because the extra mass in front of the spine shifts the center of gravity forward, and the back muscles, which have a short lever arm, must exert large forces (about $$ 392 \N $$ for $$ 10 \kg $$ held $$ 0.20 \m $$ in front of the spine) for hours each day to keep the body balanced.

:::: problem

An old carpenter’s trick to keep nails from bending when they are pounded into hard
materials is to grip the center of the nail firmly with pliers. Why does this help?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

A nail bends when the compressive force from the hammer makes it buckle sideways. The force a slender rod can carry before buckling depends strongly on its unsupported length, so ask what gripping the middle does to that length.

**Solution**

When you hammer a nail into hard wood, the wood resists, and the nail is squeezed between the hammer and the wood. A long, thin rod under compression does not just shorten; at a certain critical force it suddenly bows out sideways (buckles). That critical force is inversely proportional to the square of the unsupported length $$ L $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{crit}} \propto \frac{1}{L^{2}} $$
</div>

Gripping the center of the nail firmly with pliers stops the middle of the nail from moving sideways. This splits the nail into two shorter sections, each about $$ L/2 $$ long. The critical force becomes

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{crit}}' \propto \frac{1}{(L/2)^{2}} = 4 \times \frac{1}{L^{2}} $$
</div>

so it is about 4 times larger. If a nail with $$ 6.0 \text{ cm} $$ exposed buckles under $$ 400 \N $$, gripping it in the middle lets it carry about $$ 1600 \N $$ without bending. The pliers also keep the nail lined up with the hammer blow, so the force stays along the nail's axis instead of having a sideways component that bends it.

Gripping the center of the nail with pliers keeps the nail from bending because it halves the unsupported length that can buckle, and since the buckling force varies as $$ 1/L^{2} $$, the nail can then take about 4 times the force (for example, $$ 1600 \N $$ instead of $$ 400 \N $$) and stays aligned with the blow.

:::: problem

When a glass bottle full of vinegar warms up, both the vinegar and the glass expand,
but vinegar expands significantly more with temperature than glass. The bottle will
break if it was filled to its tightly capped lid. Explain why, and also explain how
a pocket of air above the vinegar would prevent the break. (This is the function of
the air above liquids in glass containers.)

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

Compare how much the vinegar and the bottle expand when heated. Then use the bulk modulus to find the pressure needed to squeeze the extra liquid back into the bottle. Finally, compare this with what happens when there is an easily compressed air pocket.

**Solution**

Suppose the bottle warms by $$ \Delta T = 20 \text{ °C} $$. Vinegar (mostly water) expands by a fraction of about $$ 2.1 \times 10^{-4} \text{ per °C} $$, while the glass's volume grows by only about $$ 2.7 \times 10^{-5} \text{ per °C} $$. The vinegar's volume grows more than the bottle's by a fraction of

<div class="equation">
<!-- physics-scan: temperature=interval -->
$$ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = (2.1 \times 10^{-4} \text{ °C}^{-1} - 2.7 \times 10^{-5} \text{ °C}^{-1})(20 \text{ °C}) = 3.7 \times 10^{-3} $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ \Delta P = B \frac{\Delta V}{V} = (2.2 \times 10^{9} \text{ N/m}^2)(3.7 \times 10^{-3}) = 8.1 \times 10^{6} \text{ N/m}^2 $$
</div>

**With an air pocket.** Air is very easy to compress. If the air pocket takes up $$ 2.0\% $$ of the bottle's volume, the expanding vinegar takes $$ 0.37\% $$ of the volume from it, leaving the air $$ 1.63\% $$. At constant temperature the air pressure rises only to about

<div class="equation">
$$ P = (1.0 \text{ atm}) \frac{2.0\%}{1.63\%} = 1.2 \text{ atm} $$
</div>

(the air warming up raises it a little more). The glass easily withstands this.

A completely full, capped bottle breaks because the vinegar expands more than the glass and, being nearly incompressible, would need a pressure of about $$ 8 \times 10^{6} \text{ N/m}^2 $$ (about 80 atm) to be squeezed back, whereas a small air pocket compresses easily and absorbs the extra volume with a pressure rise of only about $$ 0.2 \text{ atm} $$.

:::: problem

A farmer making grape juice fills a glass bottle to the brim and caps it tightly.
The juice expands more than the glass when it warms up, in such a way that the volume
increases by 0.2% (that is, $$ \Delta V/ V_{0}=2\times 10^{-3} $$)
relative to the space available. Calculate the magnitude of the normal force
exerted by the juice per square centimeter if its bulk modulus is $$ 1.8\times 10^{9}\text{N/m}^{2} $$, assuming the bottle does not break. In view of your
answer, do you think the bottle will survive?

::::

:::: solution

**Strategy**

The bulk modulus relates pressure change to fractional volume change: $$ B = -\Delta P/(\Delta V/V_0) $$. The juice would **increase** its volume by 0.2% if free to expand; that $$ +2 \times 10^{-3} $$ is the unconstrained thermal expansion. Because the rigid bottle prevents the increase, the juice is held at its original volume, which is equivalent to compressing the freely expanded juice by a fractional amount $$ \Delta V/V_0 = -2 \times 10^{-3} $$. That negative compressive strain is what enters the signed bulk-modulus relation, giving a positive pressure increase. The compressed juice exerts an outward normal force on the glass. We solve for the magnitude of the pressure.

**Solution**

1. Apply the bulk modulus formula:
<div class="equation">
$$ B = -\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V/V_0} $$
</div>

2. The compressive strain relative to the freely expanded state is $$ \Delta V/V_0 = -2 \times 10^{-3} $$. The resulting pressure increase is:
<div class="equation">
$$ \Delta P = -B \times \frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = -(1.8 \times 10^9 \text{ N/m}^2)(-2 \times 10^{-3}) = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ N/m}^2 $$
</div>

3. Convert to the units requested in the problem ($$ 1 \text{ cm}^2 = 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2 $$):
<div class="equation">
$$ \frac{F}{A} = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ N/m}^2 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2/\text{cm}^2 = 3.6 \times 10^{2} \text{ N/cm}^2 $$
</div>

4. In pascals, bars, and atmospheres ($$ 1 \text{ bar} = 10^5 \text{ Pa} $$, $$ 1 \text{ atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} $$):
<div class="equation">
$$ |\Delta P| = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa} = 36 \text{ bar} \approx 36 \text{ atm} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The juice pushes on the bottle with about $$ 3.6 \times 10^{2} \text{ N/cm}^2 $$ (two significant figures), or roughly 36 atmospheres. Rounded to one significant figure, as the 0.2% input might justify, this is $$ 4 \times 10^{2} \text{ N/cm}^2 $$ or about 40 bar. A typical glass bottle can withstand only about 10–20 atm before failing, so the bottle will almost certainly break. This is why wine and juice bottles are not filled completely to the brim—the headspace allows for thermal expansion without building dangerous pressures.

$$ \Delta x_{\text{lin}} = 1.6 \times 10^{2} \m $$
</div>

This is about 11 times the pole's length, so **the linear formula cannot give a physical displacement for this load**. The resultant horizontal force points along the bisector of the 90° angle (45° from each wire), but the actual deformation or failure requires further analysis.

(c) Guy wire tension:

The guy wire must provide a horizontal force component equal to $$ F_{H,net} $$ to straighten the pole:

<div class="equation">
$$ T_{guy}\sin 30° = F_{H,net} $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ T_{guy} = \frac{F_{H,net}}{\sin 30°} = \frac{5.57 \times 10^4 \N}{0.500} = 1.11 \times 10^5 \N $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The $$ 1.1 \text{ mm} $$ value in (a) estimates axial compression of a straight pole under the two line tensions. The formal $$ 1.6 \times 10^{2} \m $$ result in (b) demonstrates failure of the small-deflection approximation; it is not an actual bend. The horizontal force balance in (c) gives a guy wire tension of $$ 1.11 \times 10^5 \N $$ to cancel the sideways load on a straight pole. The guy wire also adds downward force, so a supported design must check the resulting compression, stability, and material strength.

### Glossary
{: class="glossary-title"}

deformation
: change in shape due to the application of force

Hooke’s law
: proportional relationship between the force $$ F $$ on a material and the
deformation $$ \Delta L $$ it causes, $$ F=k\Delta L $$

tensile strength
: the greatest tensile stress a material can withstand

stress
: ratio of force to area

strain
: ratio of change in length to original length

shear deformation
: deformation perpendicular to the original length of an object
