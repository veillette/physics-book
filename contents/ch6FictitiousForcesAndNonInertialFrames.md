* Describe the effects of the Coriolis force.

What do taking off in a jet airplane, turning a corner in a car, riding a
merry-go-round, and the circular motion of a tropical cyclone have in common?
Each exhibits fictitious forces—unreal forces that arise from motion and may
_seem_ real, because the observer’s frame of reference is accelerating or
rotating.

When taking off in a jet, most people would agree it feels as if you are being
pushed back into the seat as the airplane accelerates down the runway. Yet a
physicist would say that _you_ tend to remain stationary while the _seat_ pushes
forward on you, and there is no real force backward on you. An even more common
experience occurs when you make a tight curve in your car—say, to the right. You
feel as if you are thrown (that is, _forced_) toward the left relative to the
car. Again, a physicist would say that _you_ tend to keep going in a straight
line while the _car_ turns to the right, until the seat, belt, or door pushes
you to the right along with it; there is no real force on you to the left.
Recall Newton’s first law.

![In figure a, there is a girl driving a car turning toward right. A fictitious force vector is acting on her body toward left. In figure b, the actual force vector acting on the girl’s body is shown toward right.](../resources/Figure_06_04_01.jpg '(a) The car driver feels herself forced to the left relative to the car when she makes a right turn. This is a fictitious force arising from the use of the car as a frame of reference. (b) In the Earth’s frame of reference, the driver tends to continue in a straight line, obeying Newton’s first law, while the car turns to the right; the car must push her to the right to make her turn too. There is no real force to the left on the driver relative to Earth. There is a real force to the right on the car to make it turn.')
{: #Figure1}

We can reconcile these points of view by examining the frames of reference used.
Let us concentrate on people in a car. Passengers instinctively use the car as a
frame of reference, while a physicist uses Earth. The physicist chooses Earth
because it is very nearly an inertial frame of reference—one in which all forces
are real (that is, in which all forces have an identifiable physical origin). In
such a frame of reference, Newton’s laws of motion take the form given
in [Dynamics: Newton\'s Laws of Motion](./ch4Dynamics) The car is a
**non-inertial frame of reference**
because it is accelerated to the side. The force to the left sensed by car
passengers is a **fictitious force**
having no physical origin. There is nothing real pushing them left—the car, as
well as the driver, is actually accelerating to the right.

Let us now take a mental ride on a merry-go-round—specifically, a rapidly
rotating playground merry-go-round. You take the merry-go-round to be your frame
of reference because you rotate together. In that non-inertial frame, you feel a
fictitious force, named **centrifugal force**
not to be confused with centripetal force, trying to throw you off. You must
hang on tightly to counteract the centrifugal force. In Earth’s frame of
reference, there is no force trying to throw you off. Rather you must hang on to
make yourself go in a circle because otherwise you would go in a straight line,
right off the merry-go-round.

![Two top views of a counterclockwise merry-go-round. In the rotating frame, an outward fictitious centrifugal-force arrow lies on the radius through the shaded rider. In the inertial frame, a real centripetal-force arrow points from the shaded rider toward the platform center. A separate unshaded released rider follows a straight tangent and is labeled F_net = 0.](/resources/Figure_06_04_02.svg '(a) A rider on a merry-go-round feels as if he is being thrown off. (b) In an inertial frame of reference and according to Newton’s laws, it is his inertia that carries him off and not a real force (the  unshaded rider has \( F_\text{net}=0 \) and heads in a straight line). A real force, \( F_{\text{centripetal}} \) , is needed to cause a circular path. ')
{: #Figure2}

This inertial effect, carrying you away from the center of rotation if there is
no centripetal force to cause circular motion, is put to good use in
centrifuges (see [Figure 3](#Figure3)). A centrifuge spins a sample very rapidly,
as mentioned earlier in this chapter. Viewed from the rotating frame of
reference, the fictitious centrifugal force throws particles outward, hastening
their sedimentation. The greater the angular velocity, the greater the
centrifugal force. But what really happens is that the inertia of the particles
carries them along a line tangent to the circle while the test tube is forced in
a circular path by a centripetal force.

![A test tube is fitted in a centrifuge. The centrifuge is rotating toward the left. The inertial force vector on a particle inside the liquid is directed toward the left. The  centrifugal force is directed toward the bottom of the test tube. The angular velocity is marked as omega.](../resources/Figure_06_04_03.jpg 'Centrifuges use inertia to perform their task. Particles in the fluid sediment come out because their inertia carries them away from the center of rotation. The large angular velocity of the centrifuge quickens the sedimentation. Ultimately, the particles will come into contact with the test tube walls, which will then supply the centripetal force needed to make them move in a circle of constant radius.')
{: #Figure3}

Let us now consider what happens if something moves in a frame of reference that
rotates. For example, what if you slide a ball directly away from the center of
the merry-go-round, as shown in [Figure 4](#Figure4)? The ball follows a straight
path relative to Earth (assuming negligible friction) and a path curved to the
right on the merry-go-round’s surface. A person standing next to the
merry-go-round sees the ball moving straight and the merry-go-round rotating
underneath it. In the merry-go-round’s frame of reference, we explain the
apparent curve to the right by using a fictitious force, called the
**Coriolis force**, that causes the ball to curve to the right. The fictitious
Coriolis force can be used by anyone in that frame of reference to explain why
objects follow curved paths and allows us to apply Newton’s Laws in non-inertial
frames of reference.

![In the figure, a child on a merry-go-round is shown. A person slides a ball from the center from the point A toward the point B. The path covered by the ball on the merry-go-round is shown, which is a curved path. The ball reaches a point away from the point B.](../resources/Figure_06_04_04.jpg 'Looking down on the counterclockwise rotation of a merry-go-round, we see that a ball slid straight toward the edge follows a path curved to the right. The person slides the ball toward point B, starting at point A. Both points rotate to the shaded positions (A’ and B’) shown in the time that the ball follows the curved path in the rotating frame and a straight path in Earth’s frame.')
{: #Figure4}

Up until now, we have considered Earth to be an inertial frame of reference with
little or no worry about effects due to its rotation. Yet such effects _do_
exist—in the rotation of weather systems, for example. Most consequences of
Earth’s rotation can be qualitatively understood by analogy with the
merry-go-round. Viewed from above the North Pole, Earth rotates
counterclockwise, as does the merry-go-round in [Figure 4](#Figure4). As on the
merry-go-round, any motion in Earth’s northern hemisphere experiences a Coriolis
force to the right. Just the opposite occurs in the southern hemisphere; there,
the force is to the left. Because Earth’s angular velocity is small, the
Coriolis force is usually negligible, but for large-scale motions, such as wind
patterns, it has substantial effects.

The Coriolis force causes hurricanes in the northern hemisphere to rotate in the
counterclockwise direction, while the tropical cyclones (what hurricanes are
called below the equator) in the southern hemisphere rotate in the clockwise
direction. The terms hurricane, typhoon, and tropical storm are
regionally-specific names for tropical cyclones, storm systems characterized by
low pressure centers, strong winds, and heavy rains. [Figure 5](#Figure5) helps
show how these rotations take place. Air flows toward any region of low
pressure, and tropical cyclones contain particularly low pressures. Thus winds
flow toward the center of a tropical cyclone or a low-pressure weather system at
the surface. In the northern hemisphere, these inward winds are deflected to the
right, as shown in the figure, producing a counterclockwise circulation at the
surface for low-pressure zones of any type. Low pressure at the surface is
associated with rising air, which also produces cooling and cloud formation,
making low-pressure patterns quite visible from space. Conversely, wind
circulation around high-pressure zones is clockwise in the northern hemisphere
but is less visible because high pressure is associated with sinking air,
producing clear skies.

The rotation of tropical cyclones and the path of a ball on a merry-go-round can
just as well be explained by inertia and the rotation of the system underneath.
When non-inertial frames are used, fictitious forces, such as the Coriolis
force, must be invented to explain the curved path. There is no identifiable
physical source for these fictitious forces. In an inertial frame, inertia
explains the path, and no force is found to be without an identifiable source.
Either view allows us to describe nature, but a view in an inertial frame is the
simplest and truest, in the sense that all forces have real origins and
explanations.

### Section Summary

- Rotating and accelerated frames of reference are non-inertial.
- Fictitious forces, such as the Coriolis force, are needed to explain motion in
  such frames.

### Conceptual Questions

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

When a toilet is flushed or a sink is drained, the water (and other material) begins to rotate about the drain on the way down. Assuming no initial rotation and a flow initially directly straight toward the drain, explain what causes the rotation and which direction it has in the northern hemisphere. (Note that this is a small effect and in most toilets the rotation is caused by directional water jets.) Would the direction of rotation reverse if water were forced up the drain?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Analyze the motion in Earth's rotating frame, where the Coriolis force deflects moving objects to the right in the northern hemisphere. Apply it to water flowing inward from all sides toward the drain, then to water flowing outward.

**Solution**

Water moves toward the drain from all directions. In the northern hemisphere, Earth's rotation deflects each moving parcel of water **to the right** of its direction of motion. Water approaching from the south is deflected toward the east, water from the north toward the west, and so on. Each stream misses the drain slightly to its right, and together they swirl around it **counterclockwise** as seen from above. That is the same direction as the winds around a low-pressure center in a northern-hemisphere hurricane.

The effect is tiny. The Coriolis acceleration is about $$ 2\Omega v \sin\lambda $$, where $$ \Omega = 7.29 \times 10^{-5} \rads $$ is Earth's angular velocity and $$ \lambda $$ is the latitude. For water moving at $$ v = 0.10 \ms $$ at latitude $$ 45° $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ a = 2(7.29 \times 10^{-5} \rads)(0.10 \ms)(\sin 45°) = 1.0 \times 10^{-5} \mss $$
</div>

That is about a millionth of $$ g $$, so in a real sink or toilet small leftover currents and the direction of the water jets nearly always decide the swirl.

If water were forced **up** the drain, it would flow **outward** from the drain. Still deflected to the right, it would swirl **clockwise** as seen from above. Yes, the direction reverses, just as winds spiral clockwise out of a northern-hemisphere high-pressure region.

The Coriolis effect of Earth's rotation deflects the water flowing toward the drain to the right in the northern hemisphere, so it rotates counterclockwise as seen from above (although the effect, about $$ 1 \times 10^{-5} \mss $$, is usually overwhelmed by other currents), and water forced up the drain would flow outward and rotate clockwise.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Is there a real force that throws water from clothes during the spin cycle of a washing machine? Explain how the water is removed.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Describe the spin cycle in Earth's (nearly inertial) frame. Ask what force is needed to keep each drop of water moving in a circle, and what supplies it.

**Solution**

No, there is no real outward force. In the inertial frame, the clothes move in a circle because the wall of the drum pushes them **inward** with a normal force, the centripetal force. A drop of water in the fabric must also be pushed or pulled inward to stay on its circle. For a drum of radius $$ 0.25 \m $$ spinning at $$ 1000 \text{ rev/min} $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ \omega = 1000 \frac{\text{rev}}{\text{min}} \times \frac{2\pi \text{ rad}}{1 \text{ rev}} \times \frac{1 \text{ min}}{60 \s} = 105 \rads $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ a_{c} = r\omega^{2} = (0.25 \m)(105 \rads)^2 = 2.8 \times 10^{3} \mss \approx 280g $$
</div>

A $$ 1.0 \text{ g} $$ drop needs an inward force of $$ (0.0010 \kg)(2.8 \times 10^{3} \mss) = 2.8 \N $$. The only thing holding the drop is the weak adhesion between water and fibers, which cannot supply such a force. The drop therefore cannot follow the circle; it continues in a straight line, tangent to its circular path, and flies out through the holes in the drum wall. The clothes stay because the drum wall pushes them inward, but the wall has holes, and nothing pushes the water inward where it sits over a hole.

There is no real force throwing the water outward; the drum wall pushes the clothes inward to keep them circling, but the water's adhesion to the fabric cannot provide the large centripetal force needed (about $$ 2.8 \N $$ for a $$ 1.0 \text{ g} $$ drop at $$ 280g $$), so the water moves off in a straight line through the holes in the drum.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

In one amusement park ride, riders enter a large vertical barrel and stand against the wall on its horizontal floor. The barrel is spun up and the floor drops away. Riders feel as if they are pinned to the wall by a force something like the gravitational force. This is a fictitious force sensed and used by the riders to explain events in the rotating frame of reference of the barrel. Explain in an inertial frame of reference (Earth is nearly one) what pins the riders to the wall, and identify all of the real forces acting on them.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Describe the riders' motion in Earth's frame: they move in a horizontal circle at the wall, so their acceleration points toward the axis. Draw the free-body diagram with $$ +x $$ horizontally toward the axis and $$ +y $$ straight up.

**Solution**

![Free-body diagram with +x horizontally toward the axis of the barrel and +y up: the wall pushes the rider toward the axis with the normal force N along +x, which is the centripetal force; static friction from the wall f s points up and balances the weight w, which points down. The acceleration points toward the axis.](../resources/fbd_06_04_q03.svg){: .fbd}

Three real forces act on each rider:

1. **The normal force** $$ N $$ from the wall, pointing horizontally toward the axis. This is the centripetal force.
2. **Static friction** $$ f_{s} $$ from the wall, pointing up along the wall.
3. **The weight** $$ w = mg $$, pointing down.

What pins the riders to the wall is the wall itself. By inertia each rider tends to move in a straight line, and the curving wall keeps getting in the way, pushing inward to change the direction of the rider's velocity. For a $$ 60 \kg $$ rider moving at $$ 9.0 \ms $$ in a barrel of radius $$ 2.5 \m $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ x: \quad N = m\frac{v^{2}}{r} = (60 \kg)\frac{(9.0 \ms)^2}{2.5 \m} = 1940 \N $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ y: \quad f_{s} - mg = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad f_{s} = (60 \kg)(9.80 \mss) = 588 \N $$
</div>

The large normal force makes a large static friction possible. The rider does not slide down as long as $$ \mu_{s} \ge (588 \N)/(1940 \N) = 0.30 $$. In the barrel's rotating frame, the riders explain the large wall force with an outward "centrifugal" force, but no such force exists in Earth's frame.

In Earth's frame the riders are pinned because the wall pushes them toward the axis with a normal force that supplies the centripetal force (about $$ 1940 \N $$ for a $$ 60 \kg $$ rider at $$ 9.0 \ms $$ in a $$ 2.5 \m $$ barrel), and the real forces on each rider are this normal force, the upward static friction from the wall that balances the weight ($$ 588 \N $$), and the weight itself.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Action at a distance, such as is the case for gravity, was once thought to be illogical and therefore untrue. What is the ultimate determinant of the truth in physics, and why was this action ultimately accepted?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Identify how physical theories are tested, then apply this to Newton's law of gravitation with a quantitative test.

**Solution**

The ultimate determinant of truth in physics is **experiment and observation**. A theory is accepted when its predictions agree with measurements, however strange its picture of the world may seem. Many thinkers in Newton's time objected that a force acting across empty space, with nothing touching, was illogical. Newton's law of gravitation was accepted because it worked.

One of its first successes was the "Moon test." If gravity weakens as $$ 1/r^{2} $$, then at the Moon's distance, about 60.3 Earth radii, it should produce an acceleration of

<div class="equation">
$$ a = g\left(\frac{R_{E}}{r}\right)^{2} = (9.80 \mss)\left(\frac{1}{60.3}\right)^{2} = 2.70 \times 10^{-3} \mss $$
</div>

The Moon's measured centripetal acceleration, with $$ r = 3.84 \times 10^{8} \m $$ and $$ T = 27.3 \text{ d} = 2.36 \times 10^{6} \s $$, is

<div class="equation">
$$ a_{c} = \frac{4\pi^{2}r}{T^{2}} = \frac{4\pi^{2}(3.84 \times 10^{8} \m)}{(2.36 \times 10^{6} \s)^2} = 2.72 \times 10^{-3} \mss $$
</div>

in close agreement. The same law then explained Kepler's laws, the tides, and the motion of comets, and it predicted the return of Halley's comet in 1758 and led to the discovery of Neptune in 1846. Faced with such agreement, physicists accepted action at a distance. (Later, the field concept and general relativity gave a different picture of how gravity acts, which again had to pass experimental tests.)

The ultimate determinant of truth in physics is agreement with experiment and observation, and action at a distance was accepted because Newton's law of gravitation correctly predicted measured motions, such as the Moon's centripetal acceleration of about $$ 2.7 \times 10^{-3} \mss $$, the planets' orbits, the tides, and the return of comets.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Two friends are having a conversation. Anna says a satellite in orbit is in freefall because the satellite keeps falling toward Earth. Tom says a satellite in orbit is not in freefall because the acceleration due to gravity is not 9.80 $$ \mss $$. Who do you agree with and why?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use the definition of free fall: motion under the influence of gravity alone. Then check the value of $$ g $$ at a typical orbital altitude.

**Solution**

Agree with **Anna**. An object is in free fall when **gravity is the only force** acting on it, whatever the value of the gravitational acceleration. A satellite in orbit has no thrust, no support, and essentially no air resistance, so it is in free fall. It falls toward Earth continuously, but it moves sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it, so it keeps missing.

Tom is right that the acceleration is not $$ 9.80 \mss $$, but that does not matter. The value $$ 9.80 \mss $$ applies only near Earth's surface, and gravity weakens as $$ 1/r^{2} $$. For the International Space Station, about $$ 400 \text{ km} $$ above the surface, $$ r = 6.37 \times 10^{6} \m + 0.40 \times 10^{6} \m = 6.77 \times 10^{6} \m $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ g = (9.80 \mss)\left(\frac{6.37 \times 10^{6} \m}{6.77 \times 10^{6} \m}\right)^{2} = 8.68 \mss $$
</div>

The station and the astronauts inside fall with this same acceleration, which is why they float relative to one another.

Anna is right: the satellite is in free fall because gravity is the only force acting on it, and the fact that the gravitational acceleration at its altitude is smaller than $$ 9.80 \mss $$ (about $$ 8.68 \mss $$ at $$ 400 \text{ km} $$) does not change that.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

A non-rotating frame of reference placed at the center of the Sun is very nearly an inertial one. Why is it not exactly an inertial frame?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

An inertial frame is one that is not accelerating. Look for forces on the Sun that make it, and a frame attached to its center, accelerate.

**Solution**

The Sun is not exactly unaccelerated, for two main reasons.

**1. The planets pull on the Sun.** By Newton's third law, every planet attracts the Sun just as the Sun attracts the planet. Jupiter has the largest effect. Its mass is $$ 1.90 \times 10^{27} \kg $$ and its distance is $$ 7.78 \times 10^{11} \m $$, so it gives the Sun an acceleration of

<div class="equation">
$$ a = \frac{GM_{J}}{r^{2}} = \frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11} \N \cdot \mm/\kg^{2})(1.90 \times 10^{27} \kg)}{(7.78 \times 10^{11} \m)^2} = 2.1 \times 10^{-7} \mss $$
</div>

The Sun wobbles around the center of mass of the solar system.

**2. The Sun orbits the center of the Milky Way.** It moves at about $$ 2.2 \times 10^{5} \ms $$ on a circle of radius about $$ 2.5 \times 10^{20} \m $$, with a centripetal acceleration of

<div class="equation">
$$ a_{c} = \frac{v^{2}}{r} = \frac{(2.2 \times 10^{5} \ms)^2}{2.5 \times 10^{20} \m} = 1.9 \times 10^{-10} \mss $$
</div>

Both accelerations are tiny, so for most purposes the Sun-centered frame is an excellent inertial frame, but neither is exactly zero.

A non-rotating frame at the Sun's center is not exactly inertial because the Sun accelerates: the planets' gravity (mainly Jupiter's) gives it an acceleration of about $$ 2 \times 10^{-7} \mss $$, and it orbits the center of the galaxy with a centripetal acceleration of about $$ 2 \times 10^{-10} \mss $$.

::: glossary

### Glossary
{: class="glossary-title"}

fictitious force
: a force having no physical origin

centrifugal force
: a fictitious force that tends to throw an object off when the object is
rotating in a non-inertial frame of reference

Coriolis force
: the fictitious force causing the apparent deflection of moving objects when
viewed in a rotating frame of reference

non-inertial frame of reference
: an accelerated frame of reference

:::
