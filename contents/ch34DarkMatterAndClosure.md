Galaxies and galaxy clusters exert more gravity than their visible stars and
gas can explain. The additional matter inferred from this gravity is called
**dark matter**. Its effects are well established, but its composition remains
unknown.

### Evidence

In the 1930s, Fritz Zwicky found that galaxies in the Coma cluster moved too
quickly to be held together by the visible matter alone. Later, Vera Rubin and
other astronomers studied the rotation of spiral galaxies. Far from a galaxy's
bright center, stars often orbit at nearly constant speeds instead of slowing
as expected if most of the mass followed the visible light. This points to an
extended halo of unseen matter.

Gravitational lensing and the behavior of hot gas in clusters provide further
evidence. The hot gas emits X-rays and is ordinary matter; its measured mass,
together with the stars, is still insufficient to explain the cluster's gravity
(see [[Figure 1]](#Figure1)).

X-ray-emitting gas is part of the ordinary matter; the total mass inferred from gravity is larger. (credit: NASA, ESA, CXC, M. Bradac, and S. Allen)')

### Density, Curvature, and Expansion

The **critical density** $$\rho_c$$ is the total mass-equivalent energy density
corresponding to a spatially flat universe. It is about
$$10^{-26}\text{ kg/m}^3$$, equivalent to only a few protons per cubic meter.
All components, including dark energy, must be counted when comparing the
total density with this value.

In standard cosmological models, a total density below, equal to, or above the
critical density corresponds to negative, zero, or positive spatial curvature.
These geometries are often called **open**, **flat**, and **closed**. Curvature
does not, by itself, determine whether expansion will continue forever. The
future also depends on the contents of the universe and the behavior of dark
energy.

An older matter-only model linked sufficient density to a future recollapse,
or **Big Crunch**. The observed accelerated expansion changes that picture.
In the standard model with a positive **cosmological constant**, dark energy
keeps the expansion accelerating. A cosmological constant is one possible
description of dark energy, not a directly identified substance.

For scale, the Planck mission's 2013 results gave an approximate inventory of
5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy. These are fractions of
the total mass-energy density, not just of the matter. Thus dark matter is
roughly five times as abundant as ordinary matter. Observations are consistent
with nearly flat space.

### What Could Dark Matter Be?

Faint stars, planets, and stellar remnants contain ordinary matter that is hard
to see. Some can be found through **microlensing**, the temporary brightening of
a background star as an intervening object's gravity bends its light. Such
compact objects have been investigated as **massive compact halo objects
(MACHOs)**, but observations show that ordinary compact objects cannot account
for most dark matter.

![The Hubble Space Telescope in orbit.](../resources/Figure_34_04_02.jpg 'Space telescopes help study faint objects and gravitational lensing, providing evidence about the distribution of matter. (credit: NASA, crew of STS-125)')
{: #Figure2}

**Neutrino oscillations** provide an important connection with particle physics.
An electron neutrino can be detected later as a muon or tau neutrino. This
change of flavor shows that neutrinos have nonzero masses and explains the
formerly missing solar electron neutrinos. Their exact masses are still being
measured. Ordinary relic neutrinos contribute some mass, but they are too light
and move too fast to account for most dark matter while forming the structures
we observe. Hypothetical sterile neutrinos are a separate possibility.

Other proposed particles include **weakly interacting massive particles
(WIMPs)** and **axions**. WIMPs are a broad class of hypothetical massive
particles; a neutralino is one possible example. They need not be leptons.
Axions are a different class of very light, spin-zero particles with extremely
weak proposed interactions. They are not conventional WIMPs. Neither class has
been established as the source of cosmic dark matter.

![Leaves carried along a stream.](../resources/Figure_34_04_03.jpg 'An analogy: unseen motion in a stream shapes the distribution of leaves. Similarly, the gravitational influence of dark matter helps shape the distribution of visible matter. (credit: Shinichi Sugiyama)')
{: #Figure3}

### Section Summary

- Dark matter is inferred from gravity, including galaxy rotation, cluster
  motion, and gravitational lensing. Its composition is unknown.
- Critical density concerns total mass-energy density and spatial curvature.
  Geometry alone does not determine the universe's future expansion.
- Dark matter and dark energy have different roles: dark matter helps explain
  gravitational clustering; dark energy is associated with accelerated expansion.
- Neutrino oscillations and nonzero neutrino masses are established. Ordinary
  relic neutrinos cannot account for most dark matter.
- WIMPs, axions, and other proposed particles are being investigated.

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

The critical mass density needed to just halt the expansion of the universe is approximately  $$ 10^{-26} \text{kg}/{\text{m}}^{3} $$.

(a) Convert this to $$\text{eV}/{c}^{2}\cdot {\text{m}}^{3} $$.

(b) Find the number of neutrinos per cubic meter needed to close the universe if
their average mass is $$7 \text{eV}/{c}^{2} $$ and they have negligible kinetic
energies.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

(a) To convert mass density from kg/m³ to eV/c²·m³, we use the mass-energy equivalence relation $$ E = mc^{2} $$. We need to convert kg to eV/c² using the conversion factor between joules and electron volts.

(b) If neutrinos with mass $$ m_\nu = 7 \text{ eV}/c^2 $$ make up the critical density, we can find the number density by dividing the critical density (in eV/c²·m³) by the mass of a single neutrino.

**Solution**

(a) First, we need to convert 1 kg to eV/c².

The energy equivalent of 1 kg is:
<div class="equation">
$$ E = mc^{2} = 1 \text{ kg} \times (3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})^2 = 9.00 \times 10^{16} \text{ J} $$
</div>

Converting to eV (using $$ 1 \text{ eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} $$):
<div class="equation">
$$ E = \frac{9.00 \times 10^{16} \text{ J}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J/eV}} = 5.625 \times 10^{35} \text{ eV} $$
</div>

Therefore: $$ 1 \text{ kg} = 5.625 \times 10^{35} \text{ eV}/c^2 $$

Now converting the critical density:
<div class="equation">
$$ \rho_{c} = 10^{-26} \text{ kg/m}^3 \times 5.625 \times 10^{35} \frac{\text{eV}/c^2}{\text{kg}} = 5.6 \times 10^9 \text{ eV}/(c^2 \cdot \text{m}^3) $$
</div>

(b) The number density of neutrinos needed is:
<div class="equation">
$$ n = \frac{\rho_{c}}{m_\nu} = \frac{5.6 \times 10^9 \text{ eV}/(c^2 \cdot \text{m}^3)}{7 \text{ eV}/c^2} = 8.0 \times 10^8 \text{ neutrinos/m}^3 $$
</div>

**Discussion**

(a) The critical density in particle physics units is approximately **5.6 × 10⁹ eV/(c²·m³)** or about **6 × 10⁹ eV/(c²·m³)**. This conversion is useful in particle physics and cosmology because masses of elementary particles are typically expressed in eV/c² rather than kilograms. For reference, an electron has a mass of about 511,000 eV/c², so the critical density corresponds to a tiny fraction of an electron mass per cubic meter.

(b) To close the universe with neutrinos having an average mass of **7 eV/c²**, we would need approximately **8 × 10⁸ neutrinos per cubic meter**. This is an enormous number density—800 million neutrinos in every cubic meter of space! However, this is actually plausible. The cosmic neutrino background, a relic from the Big Bang similar to the cosmic microwave background, is predicted to have a number density of about 1.1 × 10⁸ neutrinos and antineutrinos per cubic meter per flavor, or about 3.4 × 10⁸ in total across three flavors. This is below the 8 × 10⁸ per cubic meter required by this hypothetical 7-eV calculation. Massive neutrinos were historically considered as a dark-matter candidate.

However, modern measurements suggest that neutrino masses are too small (probably less than 1 eV/c² for the heaviest flavor) to account for all dark matter, though they contribute a small fraction. The neutrino oscillation experiments that confirmed neutrinos have mass represent one of the most important discoveries in particle physics, showing physics beyond the Standard Model. While neutrinos don't solve the dark matter mystery completely, they remain an important component of the universe's mass-energy budget.

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

To get an idea of how empty deep space is on the average, perform the following calculations:

(a) Find the volume our Sun would occupy if it had an average density equal to
the critical density of $$ 10^{-26} \text{kg}/{\text{m}}^{3} $$ thought
necessary to halt the expansion of the universe.

(b) Find the radius of a sphere of this volume in light years.

(c) What would this radius be if the density were that of luminous matter, which
is approximately $$5\% $$ that of the critical density?

(d) Compare the radius found in part (c) with the 4-ly average separation of
stars in the arms of the Milky Way.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

(a) We'll use the relationship $$ V = M/\rho $$ with the Sun's mass and the critical density to find the volume.

(b) For a sphere, $$ V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 $$, so we can solve for $$ r $$ and convert to light years.

(c) We'll repeat part (b) with 5% of the critical density.

(d) We'll calculate the ratio of the radius from part (c) to the 4-ly stellar separation.

**Solution**

(a) The volume the Sun's mass would occupy at critical density:
<div class="equation">
$$ V = \frac{M_{\text{Sun}}}{\rho_{c}} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{30} \text{ kg}}{10^{-26} \text{ kg/m}^3} = 2.0 \times 10^{56} \text{ m}^3 $$
</div>

(b) For a sphere, $$ V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 $$, so:
<div class="equation">
$$ r^3 = \frac{3V}{4\pi} = \frac{3 \times 2.0 \times 10^{56} \text{ m}^3}{4\pi} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{56} \text{ m}^3}{12.566} = 4.77 \times 10^{55} \text{ m}^3 $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ r = (4.77 \times 10^{55} \text{ m}^3)^{1/3} = 3.63 \times 10^{18} \text{ m} $$
</div>

Converting to light years (using $$ 1 \text{ ly} = 9.46 \times 10^{15} \text{ m} $$):
<div class="equation">
$$ r = \frac{3.63 \times 10^{18} \text{ m}}{9.46 \times 10^{15} \text{ m/ly}} = 384 \text{ ly} $$
</div>

(c) If the density is 5% of critical density:
<div class="equation">
$$ \rho = 0.05 \times 10^{-26} \text{ kg/m}^3 = 5 \times 10^{-28} \text{ kg/m}^3 $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ V = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{30} \text{ kg}}{5 \times 10^{-28} \text{ kg/m}^3} = 4.0 \times 10^{57} \text{ m}^3 $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ r^3 = \frac{3 \times 4.0 \times 10^{57} \text{ m}^3}{4\pi} = 9.55 \times 10^{56} \text{ m}^3 $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ r = (9.55 \times 10^{56} \text{ m}^3)^{1/3} = 9.85 \times 10^{18} \text{ m} $$
</div>

Converting to light years:
<div class="equation">
$$ r = \frac{9.85 \times 10^{18} \text{ m}}{9.46 \times 10^{15} \text{ m/ly}} = 1.04 \times 10^{3} \text{ ly} $$
</div>

(d) Comparing with the 4-ly stellar separation:
<div class="equation">
$$ \frac{r}{d_{\text{stars}}} = \frac{1040 \text{ ly}}{4 \text{ ly}} = 260 $$
</div>

The radius is approximately **260 times** larger than the average stellar separation.

**Discussion**

These calculations dramatically illustrate just how empty the universe is on average:

(a-b) If the Sun's mass were spread out at the critical density of the universe, it would occupy a sphere with a radius of **384 light years**—roughly 100 times the distance to the nearest star! This shows that even the "critical density" required to close the universe is astonishingly low. Space is incredibly empty.

(c) When we use the density of luminous matter (only 5% of critical density), the required radius increases to about **1040 light years**. This is about the thickness of the disk of our Milky Way galaxy. Imagine spreading the Sun's mass uniformly throughout a sphere this large—that gives you an idea of how sparse luminous matter is in the universe.

(d) The fact that this radius is **260 times** the typical 4-ly separation between stars in our galactic neighborhood demonstrates a profound truth: stars and galaxies are like tiny islands of dense matter in an overwhelmingly empty universe. The average density of the universe is incomprehensibly lower than even the sparse density of interstellar space within our galaxy.

This exercise helps us appreciate why cosmologists say the universe is "matter dominated" even though space is nearly empty. The critical density—the density needed to eventually halt cosmic expansion through gravity—is so low that it corresponds to only about 6 protons per cubic meter of space. By comparison, the air you're breathing contains about 10²⁵ molecules per cubic meter! Yet even this incredibly low critical density is higher than the density of luminous matter we actually observe, which is why dark matter is necessary to explain the universe's dynamics. The emptiness of space on cosmic scales is truly difficult for our everyday intuition to grasp.

::: glossary

### Glossary
{: class="glossary-title"}

axions
: hypothetical very light spin-zero particles that are dark-matter candidates

cosmological constant
: a constant contribution to the energy density of space, one possible form of dark energy

critical density
: the total mass-equivalent energy density corresponding to spatially flat space

dark matter
: indirectly observed non-luminous matter

flat (zero curvature) universe
: a universe with zero large-scale spatial curvature

microlensing
: the temporary brightening of a background star as an intervening object gravitationally bends its light

MACHOs
: massive compact halo objects, such as faint stars or stellar remnants

neutrino oscillations
: changes in the detected flavor of a propagating neutrino

neutralinos
: hypothetical neutral particles that can be WIMP candidates in some theories

negatively curved
: having an open spatial geometry; curvature alone does not determine the future expansion

positively curved
: having a closed spatial geometry; curvature alone does not require eventual recollapse

WIMPs
: weakly interacting massive particles, a broad class of hypothetical dark-matter candidates

:::
