* Describe the distinction between renewable and nonrenewable energy sources.
* Explain why the inevitable conversion of energy to less useful forms makes it necessary to conserve energy resources.

:::

Energy is an important ingredient in all phases of society. Access to adequate
and reliable energy resources is crucial for economic activity and quality of
life. The sources of that energy also affect air quality, climate, and energy
security. In 2025, oil supplied about one-third of the world’s commercially
tracked total energy supply, and the United States accounted for about 18% of
global oil consumption by energy content. These figures come from the
[Energy Institute’s *Statistical Review of World Energy 2026*](https://www.energyinst.org/statistical-review/home).

### Renewable and Nonrenewable Energy Sources

The principal energy resources used in the world are shown in [Figure 1](#Figure1).
Oil remains the largest individual source. Together, the nonrenewable
**fossil fuels**—oil, natural gas, and coal—supplied about 86% of the energy
covered by this dataset in 2025. **Renewable forms of energy** come from
sources that nature replenishes on human timescales, such as flowing water,
wind, sunlight, and biomass. Renewable resources still need careful use: a
forest, for example, can be depleted if trees are cut faster than they regrow.
Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases released by human activities,
chiefly the burning of fossil fuels, are causing global warming. Reducing
these emissions requires changes in energy sources as well as more efficient
energy use.

#### How energy accounting changes the picture

Beginning with its 2025 edition, the Energy Institute adopted the **physical
energy content** method for reporting **total energy supply (TES)**. Earlier
editions used a **fossil-fuel-equivalent** method: electricity from non-fossil
sources was expressed as the fuel input a reference fossil-fuel power plant
would have needed to generate it. Under the new method, wind, solar
photovoltaic (PV), and hydroelectric energy enter the accounts as the electricity
they generate, without adding hypothetical thermal power plant losses.

For example, 1 kWh of wind-generated electricity counts as 3.6 MJ in TES.
A fossil-fuel-equivalent calculation assuming a 40% efficient reference plant
would count that same electricity as 9.0 MJ of fuel input. For nuclear power,
the physical energy content method counts the heat supplied to the turbines;
the Energy Institute estimates this using a 33% conversion efficiency.
Combustible biomass and other thermal sources also require heat-input
accounting. Counting wind, solar PV, and hydro at their electrical output is
an accounting convention, not a claim that their devices capture all the
energy in the wind, sunlight, or flowing water.

This change reduces the reported contribution of several renewables and the
reported energy total relative to the older method, increasing the calculated
fossil-fuel share even when physical production is unchanged. Thus the 86%
shown here cannot be compared directly with the roughly 83% shown in this
section’s former 2020 chart. Figures 1–2, [Table 1](#Table1), and the energy
axis of [Figure 4](#Figure4) all use the revised method. The Energy Institute
series covers commercially traded fuels, modern renewable electricity, and
biofuels; it excludes traditional biomass such as wood and dung collected for
household use. TES includes energy used or lost in conversion, so it differs
from both electricity generation and the energy finally delivered to users.
See the [Energy Institute’s methodology explanation](https://www.energyinst.org/statistical-review/about).

### The World’s Growing Energy Needs

[Figure 2](#Figure2) shows reported world total energy supply from 2015 through
2025, with every year calculated on the same revised basis. Annual supply rose
from 523.80 EJ to 600.31 EJ, an increase of about 15%, despite the decline in
2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. These are historical estimates, including
2025, rather than projections; future demand depends on economic growth,
technology, efficiency, and policy.

In its separate [*Global Energy Review 2026*](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends),
the International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that global energy demand grew
by 1.3% in 2025. Solar PV supplied more than one-quarter of that growth, the
largest contribution from any single source. The IEA’s broader coverage,
including traditional biomass, and its estimates differ from the Energy
Institute series, so the two sources’ totals and growth rates should not be
interchanged. The Energy Institute also adjusts its published growth rates for
leap years, whereas Figure 2 plots the reported annual energy totals.

### Energy and Economic Well-being

Energy use per person varies widely between countries. Higher GDP per person
generally accompanies higher energy use per person, as illustrated by the
selected countries in [Figure 4](#Figure4). GDP is adjusted for **purchasing
power parity (PPP)**, which accounts for differences in price levels between
countries, and expressed in constant 2021 international dollars. Both axes
use 2025 data. Energy is annual TES per person, converted from gigajoules to
kilowatt-hours using 1 kWh = 0.0036 GJ; it includes fuels as well as electricity.
A kilowatt-hour measures energy, whereas a kilowatt measures power.

The association is not a fixed requirement: countries with similar incomes can
use different amounts of energy because of climate, industrial activity,
transport, and efficiency. For example, Germany has higher GDP per person
than Russia but lower energy use per person. This comparison does not by
itself establish causation. Improving well-being while limiting environmental
damage requires attention to both energy sources and how effectively energy
is used.

New and diversified energy sources can also affect economic opportunity and stability. Renewable energy has become a large and growing source of employment. A mix of energy sources can make a power system more resilient, because a disruption to one fuel supply or one type of plant does not shut down everything. The 21st century has already seen major economic impacts from energy disruptions: Hurricane Katrina, Superstorm Sandy, various wildfires, Hurricane Maria, and the 2021 Texas winter storm demonstrated the vulnerability of United States power systems. (During the Texas storm, natural gas, coal, nuclear, and wind generation all failed in the extreme cold, so resilience depends on how every source is designed and maintained.) Distributed generation, such as rooftop solar with battery storage and local microgrids, can keep critical services running when the main grid fails. And as critical information infrastructure, such as data centers, requires more of the world's energy, supplying those growing systems during normal operations and crises will be increasingly important.

### Conserving Energy

As we finish this chapter on energy and work, it is relevant to draw some
distinctions between two sometimes misunderstood terms in the area of energy
use. As has been mentioned elsewhere, the “law of the conservation of energy” is
a very useful principle in analyzing physical processes. It is a very good bookkeeping
device, it can be traced to the fact that the laws of physics do not change with
time, and no exceptions have ever been found. It states that the total amount of
energy in an isolated system will always remain constant. Related to this
principle, but remarkably different from it, is the important philosophy of
energy conservation. This concept has to do with seeking to decrease the amount
of energy used by an individual or group through (1) reduced activities (e.g.,
turning down thermostats, driving fewer kilometers) and/or (2) increasing
conversion efficiencies in the performance of a particular task—such as
developing and using more efficient room heaters, cars that have greater
miles-per-gallon ratings, energy-efficient LED lights, etc.

Since energy in an isolated system is not destroyed or created or generated, one
might wonder why we need to be concerned about our energy resources, since
energy is a conserved quantity. The problem is that the final result of most
energy transformations is waste heat transfer to the environment and conversion
to energy forms no longer useful for doing work. To state it in another way, the
potential for energy to produce useful work has been “degraded” in the energy
transformation.

### Section Summary

- Fossil fuels supplied about 86% of the commercially tracked total energy
  supply in 2025 under physical energy content accounting. This percentage
  cannot be compared directly with shares calculated using the older
  fossil-fuel-equivalent method.
- The physical energy content method counts wind, solar PV, and hydroelectric
  electricity at their electrical output, while thermal sources such as
  nuclear power are counted at their heat input.
- Renewable shares depend on what is measured: hydroelectricity and other
  renewables supplied about 7.2% of U.S. TES in [Table 1](#Table1), while
  renewables generated about 24% of U.S. utility-scale electricity in 2025.
- Higher GDP per person generally accompanies higher annual energy use per
  person, but climate, industry, transport, and efficiency also matter.
- Even though energy can never be created or destroyed, real energy-use
  processes commonly convert some energy into less useful forms, such as
  waste heat released to the environment.

### Conceptual Questions

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

What is the difference between energy conservation and the law of conservation of energy? Give some examples of each.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Distinguish between the physical law (the total energy of an isolated system is constant) and the everyday practice (reducing the energy we use from sources such as fuels).

**Solution**

The **law of conservation of energy** is a law of physics: energy is never created or destroyed, only transferred or changed in form, so the total energy of an isolated system stays constant. It always holds. Examples:

- A $$ 2.0 \kg $$ rock falling $$ 10 \m $$ converts $$ (2.0 \kg)(9.80 \mss)(10 \m) = 196 \J $$ of gravitational potential energy into $$ 196 \J $$ of kinetic energy (ignoring air resistance).
- In a $$ 35\% $$ efficient power plant, every $$ 100 \J $$ of chemical energy in coal becomes $$ 35 \J $$ of electrical energy plus $$ 65 \J $$ of thermal energy: still $$ 100 \J $$ in total.

**Energy conservation** is a practice: using less of our energy resources (fuels, electricity) to do the same tasks, by wasting less or improving efficiency. Energy is not destroyed when we waste it, but it ends up as low-temperature thermal energy that we cannot use again. Examples:

- Replacing a $$ 60 \text{ W} $$ incandescent bulb with a $$ 9 \text{ W} $$ LED bulb giving the same light saves $$ 51 \text{ W} $$, or $$ (0.051 \text{ kW})(4 \text{ h}) = 0.20 \text{ kW} \cdot \text{h} $$ for 4 hours of use each day.
- Insulating a house, carpooling, and turning off unused devices.

The law of conservation of energy is a law of physics stating that the total energy of an isolated system never changes (a $$ 35\% $$ efficient plant still accounts for all $$ 100 \J $$ of every $$ 100 \J $$ it uses), whereas energy conservation is the practice of using less of our energy resources, for example by replacing a $$ 60 \text{ W} $$ bulb with a $$ 9 \text{ W} $$ LED.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

If the efficiency of a coal-fired electrical generating plant is 35%, then what do we mean when we say that energy is a conserved quantity?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use the definition of efficiency and account for all of the energy that goes into the plant.

**Solution**

An efficiency of $$ 35\% $$ means that $$ 35\% $$ of the chemical energy released by burning the coal becomes **electrical energy**, the useful output. The other $$ 65\% $$ is not destroyed. It leaves the plant as **thermal energy**, mostly carried away in the cooling water and the hot exhaust gases, with a little lost as sound and through friction.

For each $$ 1000 \J $$ of chemical energy in the coal:

<div class="equation">
$$ E_{\text{in}} = 1000 \J = \underbrace{350 \J}_{\text{electrical}} + \underbrace{650 \J}_{\text{thermal}} $$
</div>

The total energy is the same before and after: energy is **conserved**. The low efficiency means only that most of the energy ends up in a form, low-temperature heat, that we cannot easily use.

Saying that energy is conserved means that all the chemical energy released by the coal is accounted for: in a $$ 35\% $$ efficient plant, each $$ 1000 \J $$ becomes $$ 350 \J $$ of electrical energy and $$ 650 \J $$ of thermal energy released to the surroundings, so none is created or destroyed.

:::

</div>

(a) Calculate the force the woman in [Figure 5](#Figure5) exerts to do a push-up at
constant speed, taking all data to be known to three digits.
(b) How much work does she do if her center of mass rises 0.240 m?
(c) What is her useful power output if she does 25 push-ups in 1 min?

work

.

![A woman is doing push-ups. Her weight w is acting on her center of gravity , shown by a vector pointing downwards. Her center of gravity  is zero point nine zero meters from her feet and reaction force F acting on her arms is shown by the vector pointing upward along her arms. The distance of reaction force from the feet is one point five zero meters.](../resources/Figure_07_09_05.jpg 'Forces involved in doing push-ups. The woman’s weight acts as a force exerted downward on her center of gravity (CG).')
{: #Figure5}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

For part (a), use torque equilibrium about the feet to find the force on the arms. From the figure, her mass is 50.0 kg, the center of gravity is 0.900 m from the feet, and the arms support the body at 1.50 m from the feet. For part (b), calculate work done against gravity as the center of mass rises. For part (c), find power from total work done in a given time.

**Sketch and coordinate system**

![The woman’s body is a rigid plank pivoting at her feet. +x points to the right (from feet toward head), +y points up, and counterclockwise torques are positive. Her weight w = 490 N acts down at 0.900 m from the feet, giving a clockwise (negative) torque; the floor pushes up on her hands with F at 1.50 m from the feet, giving a counterclockwise (positive) torque.](../resources/sketch_07_09_p01.svg){: .sketch}

Take $$ +x $$ from the feet toward the head, $$ +y $$ upward, and counterclockwise torques as positive, with the pivot at the feet. The upward force $$ F $$ on the hands then gives a positive torque $$ +F(1.50\m) $$ and the weight gives a negative torque $$ -w(0.900\m) $$.

**Solution**

**(a)** Taking torques about the feet (where normal force acts), with counterclockwise positive:

<div class="equation">
$$ \sum \tau = 0 $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ F(1.50\m) - w(0.90\m) = 0 $$
</div>

Her weight is:

<div class="equation">
$$ w = mg = (50.0\kg)(9.80\mss) = 490\N $$
</div>

Solving for F:

<div class="equation">
$$ F = \frac{0.90\m}{1.50\m} w = 0.600(490\N) = 294\N $$
</div>

The force she exerts on the floor with her arms is 294 N.

**(b)** Work done raising her center of mass 0.240 m:

<div class="equation">
$$ W = mgh = (490\N)(0.240\m) = 118\J $$
</div>

**(c)** Power output for 25 push-ups in 1.00 min (60.0 s):

Total work for 25 push-ups:

<div class="equation">
$$ W_{\text{total}} = 25 \times 118\J = 2950\J $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ P = \frac{W_{\text{total}}}{t} = \frac{2950\J}{60.0\s} = 49.2\text{W} \approx 49.0\text{W} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The force exerted by the woman's arms (294 N) is 60% of her body weight, which makes sense given the lever arm geometry. The work done per push-up (118 J) accounts for raising her center of mass, and the power output of approximately 49 W is reasonable for sustained exercise. Note that work done lowering the body is not included in useful work output, as discussed in the Work, Energy, and Power in Humans section—the muscles are doing negative work while lowering, which doesn't count toward useful power output.

**Answer**

(a) The woman exerts a force of **294 N** on the floor with her arms.

(b) She does **118 J** of work per push-up.

(c) Her useful power output is **49.0 W**.

::: problem

**Integrated Concepts**

A 75.0-kg cross-country skier is climbing a $$ 3.0^\circ $$ slope at a constant
speed of 2.00 m/s and encounters air resistance of 25.0 N. Find his power output
for work done against the gravitational force and air resistance.
(b) What average force does he exert backward on the snow to accomplish this?
(c) If he continues to exert this force and to experience the same air
resistance when he reaches a level area, how long will it take him to reach a
velocity of 10.0 m/s?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

For part (a), at constant speed, the skier must exert force to overcome both the component of gravity down the slope and air resistance. Power is $$ P = Fv $$.

**Sketch and coordinate system**

![Two panels. (a) On the 3.0° slope (drawn steeper): +x points up the slope, the direction of motion. The snow pushes the skier forward with F along +x (the reaction to his backward push); air resistance F_air = 25.0 N and the weight component mg sin θ point along −x. He moves at a constant 2.00 m/s. (c) On level snow: +x points in the direction of motion; F along +x and F_air along −x; the speed rises from 2.00 m/s to 10.0 m/s.](../resources/sketch_07_09_p02.svg){: .sketch}

On the slope take $$ +x $$ up the incline, the direction of motion. The snow's forward push $$ F $$ on the skier points along $$ +x $$, while air resistance and the weight component $$ F_g = mg\sin{\theta} $$ point along $$ -x $$; at constant speed, $$ F - F_g - F_{\text{air}} = 0 $$. On the level, $$ +x $$ is still the direction of motion and $$ F_{\text{net}} = F - F_{\text{air}} $$.

**Solution**

**(a)** Force components and power output:

Force components:

- Gravity down slope: $$ F_g = mg\sin{\theta} = (75.0\kg)(9.80\mss)\sin{3.0^\circ} = 38.5\N $$
- Air resistance: $$ F_{\text{air}} = 25.0\N $$

Total force needed:

<div class="equation">
 $$ F = F_g + F_{\text{air}} = 38.5\N + 25.0\N = 63.5\N $$
</div>

Power output:

<div class="equation">
 $$ P = Fv = (63.5\N)(2.00\ms) = 127\text{ W} $$
</div>

**(b)** Force exerted backward on the snow:

The force exerted backward on the snow equals the total force needed (from Newton's third law):

<div class="equation">
 $$ F = 63.5\N \approx 64\N $$
</div>

**(c)** Time to reach 10.0 m/s on level ground:

On level ground, the net force is:

<div class="equation">
 $$ F_{\text{net}} = F - F_{\text{air}} = 63.5\N - 25.0\N = 38.5\N $$
</div>

Using $$ F = ma $$:

<div class="equation">
 $$ a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{38.5\N}{75.0\kg} = 0.513\mss $$
</div>

Time to reach 10.0 m/s from 2.00 m/s:

<div class="equation">
 $$ t = \frac{\Delta v}{a} = \frac{10.0\ms - 2.00\ms}{0.513\mss} = 15.6\s $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The power output of 127 W is reasonable for sustained moderate exercise like cross-country skiing—it's roughly equivalent to a 150-W light bulb, which is sustainable for extended periods. The force of approximately 64 N (about 14 lbs) that the skier must push backward is relatively modest, showing that the primary effort is overcoming the gravitational component rather than air resistance at this moderate speed.

Part (c) reveals an interesting result: when the skier reaches level ground while maintaining the same pushing force, the acceleration becomes 0.513 m/s², and it takes 15.6 seconds to reach 10.0 m/s. This demonstrates how much easier it is to maintain speed on level ground compared to climbing a slope—the same force that barely maintains constant speed on the 3° slope produces significant acceleration on flat terrain.

**Answer**

(a) The skier's power output is **127 W**.

(b) The force exerted backward on the snow is approximately **64 N**.

(c) It will take approximately **15.6 seconds** to accelerate to 10.0 m/s on level ground.

::: problem

**Integrated Concepts**

The 70.0-kg swimmer in [Figure 6](#Figure6) starts a race with an initial velocity
of 1.25 m/s and exerts an average force of 80.0 N backward with his arms during
each 1.80 m long stroke.
(a) What is his initial acceleration if water resistance is 45.0 N?
(b) What is the subsequent average resistance force from the water during the
5.00 s it takes him to reach his top velocity of 2.50 m/s?
(c) Discuss whether water resistance seems to increase linearly with velocity.

![A person swimming and his arm motion in each stroke is represented. The arm action is shown by an arc starting from where his hand enters the surface of the water and ending at the point where his hand emerges from the water. The diameter of this arc made by the person’s hand tip in one stroke is one point eight meters.](../resources/Figure_07_09_06.jpg 'A person swimming with a stroke of 1.80m')
{: #Figure6}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use Newton's second law for parts (a) and (b). For part (a), find the initial acceleration. For part (b), use kinematics and work-energy considerations.

**Sketch and coordinate system**

![A horizontal +x axis points in the direction the swimmer moves. The water pushes him forward with F = 80.0 N along +x (the reaction to the backward push of his arms); water resistance, 45.0 N at the start, points along −x. His speed rises from 1.25 m/s to 2.50 m/s in 5.00 s.](../resources/sketch_07_09_p03.svg){: .sketch}

Take $$ +x $$ in the direction the swimmer moves. The water's forward push on the swimmer (the reaction to his 80.0-N backward push) points along $$ +x $$ and the resistance along $$ -x $$, so $$ F_{\text{net}} = 80.0\N - F_{\text{resist}} $$.

**Solution**

**(a)** Initial net force and acceleration:

The swimmer exerts 80.0 N backward, water resists with 45.0 N:

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{net}} = 80.0\N - 45.0\N = 35.0\N $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{35.0\N}{70.0\kg} = 0.500\mss $$
</div>

**(b)** Average resistance during acceleration phase:

Using kinematics, find average acceleration from 1.25 m/s to 2.50 m/s in 5.00 s:

<div class="equation">
$$ a_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{2.50\ms - 1.25\ms}{5.00\s} = 0.250\mss $$
</div>

The net force is:

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{net}} = ma = (70.0\kg)(0.250\mss) = 17.5\N $$
</div>

Average resistance force:

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{resist}} = F_{\text{applied}} - F_{\text{net}} = 80.0\N - 17.5\N = 62.5\N $$
</div>

**(c)** The 45.0 N resistance is given at the initial speed of 1.25 m/s, whereas 62.5 N is only the **time average** over the 5.00 s interval while speed rises to 2.50 m/s—it is not the instantaneous drag at top speed. Comparing 62.5 N to 45.0 N does **not** show that drag more than doubled (62.5 N is well below 90 N, twice the initial 45.0 N). If “top velocity” means the swimmer eventually reaches a steady speed with the same 80.0 N arm thrust, the drag at that terminal speed would be 80.0 N, which still does not by itself fix how drag varies with speed between 1.25 and 2.50 m/s. These two numbers alone do **not** establish whether water resistance is linear in $$ v $$, proportional to $$ v^{2} $$, or follows some other law.

**Discussion**

The initial acceleration of 0.500 m/s² is significant but manageable for a competitive swimmer. The time-averaged resistance of 62.5 N during the acceleration interval reflects that drag generally rises as speed increases, but without knowing the instantaneous drag at 2.50 m/s (or a model such as $$ F_{\text{d}} \propto v^{2} $$ fitted to several speeds), part (c) cannot support a quantitative claim about the drag–speed relation. At higher swimming speeds, drag becomes the dominant limiting factor, which is why swimmers focus on streamlining techniques to reduce resistance.

**Answer**

(a) The swimmer's initial acceleration is **0.500 m/s²**.

(b) The average water resistance during the acceleration phase is **62.5 N**.

(c) The given data **do not by themselves determine** how water resistance depends on velocity; 62.5 N is a time average, not the drag at 2.50 m/s.

**Integrated Concepts**

A toy gun uses a spring with a force constant of 300 N/m to propel a 10.0-g
steel ball. If the spring is compressed 7.00 cm and friction is negligible: (a)
How much force is needed to compress the spring?
(b) To what maximum height can the ball be shot?
(c) At what angles above the horizontal may a child aim to hit a target 3.00 m
away at the same height as the gun?
(d) What is the gun's maximum range on level ground?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

The spring's potential energy converts to kinetic energy, which then converts to potential and/or projectile kinetic energy.

**Solution**

**(a)** Maximum force (at full compression):

<div class="equation">
 $$ F = kx = (300\text{ N/m})(0.0700\m) = 21.0\N $$
</div>

**(b)** Maximum height the ball can reach:

Spring potential energy:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \PE_{\text{spring}} = \frac{1}{2}kx^{2} = \frac{1}{2}(300\text{ N/m})(0.0700\m)^{2} = 0.735\J $$
</div>

At maximum height, all energy is gravitational PE:

<div class="equation">
$$ \begin{array}{lll}
mgh &=& \PE_{\text{spring}}\\
h &=& \frac{0.735\J}{(0.0100\kg)(9.80\mss)} = 7.50\m
\end{array} $$
</div>

**(c)** Angles to hit a target 3.00 m away:

Initial velocity from spring energy:

<div class="equation">
 $$ v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2\PE_{\text{spring}}}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(0.735\J)}{0.0100\kg}} = 12.1\ms $$
</div>

For projectile motion with same initial and final height:

<div class="equation">
 $$ R = \frac{v_0^{2}\sin{2\theta}}{g} $$
</div>

Solving for angle when $$ R = 3.00\m $$:

<div class="equation">
$$ \begin{array}{lll}
\sin{2\theta} &=& \frac{Rg}{v_0^{2}} = \frac{(3.00\m)(9.80\mss)}{(12.1\ms)^{2}} = 0.201\\
2\theta &=& 11.6^\circ \text{ or } 168.4^\circ\\
\theta &=& 5.8^\circ \text{ or } 84.2^\circ
\end{array} $$
</div>

**(d)** Maximum range on level ground:

Maximum range occurs at $$ \theta = 45^\circ $$:

<div class="equation">
 $$ R_{\max} = \frac{v_0^{2}}{g} = \frac{2\PE_{\text{spring}}}{mg} = \frac{2(0.735\J)}{(0.0100\kg)(9.80\mss)} = 15.0\m $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The maximum force of 21.0 N (about 4.7 lbs) is easily achievable for compressing a toy gun spring. The maximum height of 7.50 m is impressive for a toy—equivalent to a 2.5-story building! The two possible angles to hit a target (5.8° and 84.2°) demonstrate a fundamental property of projectile motion: any target within range can be hit at two complementary angles, one nearly horizontal and one nearly vertical. The low-angle shot reaches the target quickly, while the high-angle shot has a much longer flight time. The maximum range of 15.0 m (about 49 feet) shows this is quite a powerful toy gun, emphasizing why such toys need appropriate safety precautions.

**Answer**

(a) A force of **21.0 N** is needed to fully compress the spring.

(b) The ball can reach a maximum height of **7.50 m** when shot straight up.

(c) The child can aim at **5.8° or 84.2°** above horizontal to hit the target.

(d) The gun's maximum range on level ground is **15.0 m**.

(a) What force must be supplied by an elevator cable to produce an acceleration
of $$ 0.800\mss $$ against a 200-N frictional force, if the mass of the loaded
elevator is 1500 kg?
(b) How much work is done by the cable in lifting the elevator 20.0 m?
(c) What is the final speed of the elevator if it starts from rest?
(d) How much work went into thermal energy?

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use Newton's second law for part (a). For parts (b)-(d), use work-energy relationships.

**Sketch and coordinate system**

![A vertical +y axis points up, with the origin at the elevator’s starting position (at rest) and a tick at 20.0 m. Forces on the elevator: cable force F_cable along +y, weight w = mg and friction f = 200 N along −y. **Solution**

**(a)** Using Newton's second law (upward positive):

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{cable}} - mg - f = ma $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{cable}} = m(g + a) + f = (1500\kg)(9.80\mss + 0.800\mss) + 200\N $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{cable}} = (1500\kg)(10.6\mss) + 200\N = 15900\N + 200\N = 16.1 \times 10^{3}\N $$
</div>

**(b)** Work done by the cable:

<div class="equation">
$$ W_{\text{cable}} = F_{\text{cable}} \cdot d = (16.1 \times 10^{3}\N)(20.0\m) = 3.22 \times 10^{5}\J $$
</div>

**(c)** Final speed using kinematics ($$ v_{0} = 0 $$):

<div class="equation">
$$ v^{2} = v_{0}^{2} + 2ad = 0 + 2(0.800\mss)(20.0\m) = 32.0\text{ m}^{2}/\text{s}^{2} $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ v = 5.66\ms $$
</div>

**(d)** Work done against friction (thermal energy):

<div class="equation">
$$ W_{\text{friction}} = fd = (200\N)(20.0\m) = 4.00 \times 10^{3}\J = 4.00\text{ kJ} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The cable must exert 16,100 N, which is about 1.1 times the elevator's weight, to provide the upward acceleration against friction. The total work of 322 kJ can be broken down into components: gravitational potential energy (294 kJ), kinetic energy (24 kJ), and thermal energy from friction (4.00 kJ). The final speed of 5.66 m/s (about 12.7 mph) is typical for elevators, providing a balance between efficiency and passenger comfort. The relatively small amount of energy lost to friction (about 1.2% of total work) indicates that modern elevator systems are quite efficient, though in practice, additional energy losses occur in the motor and cable system.

**Answer**

(a) The elevator cable must supply a force of **16,100 N** (or **16.1 kN**).

(b) The cable does **322 kJ** of work in lifting the elevator 20.0 m.

(c) The final speed of the elevator is **5.66 m/s**.

(d) **4.00 kJ** of work is converted to thermal energy due to friction.

**Unreasonable Results**

A car advertisement claims that its 900-kg car accelerated from rest to 30.0 m/s
and drove 100 km, gaining 3.00 km in altitude, on 1.0 gal of gasoline. The
average force of friction including air resistance was 700 N. Assume all values
are known to three significant figures.
(a) Calculate the car's efficiency.
(b) What is unreasonable about the result?
(c) Which premise is unreasonable, or which premises are inconsistent?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Calculate the total energy needed (kinetic + potential + work against friction) and compare to the energy available from 1.0 gallon of gasoline ($$ 1.2 \times 10^{8} $$ J from Table 1 in Conservation of Energy).

**Solution**

**(a)** Energy components needed:

Kinetic energy:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \frac{1}{2}(900\kg)(30.0\ms)^{2} = 4.05 \times 10^{5}\J $$
</div>

Potential energy gained:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \PE = mgh = (900\kg)(9.80\mss)(3000\m) = 2.65 \times 10^{7}\J $$
</div>

Work done against friction:

<div class="equation">
 $$ W_{f} = fd = (700\N)(100000\m) = 7.00 \times 10^{7}\J $$
</div>

Total energy needed:

<div class="equation">
 $$ E_{\text{total}} = \KE + \PE + W_{f} = 4.05 \times 10^{5} \J + 2.65 \times 10^{7} \J + 7.00 \times 10^{7} \J = 9.69 \times 10^{7}\J $$
</div>

Energy available from gasoline:

<div class="equation">
 $$ E_{\text{available}} = 1.2 \times 10^{8}\J $$
</div>

Efficiency:

<div class="equation">
 $$ \text{Eff} = \frac{E_{\text{total}}}{E_{\text{available}}} = \frac{9.69 \times 10^{7}\J}{1.2 \times 10^{8}\J} = 0.808 = 80.8\% $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The calculated efficiency of 81% is unreasonably high for a gasoline car. Real gasoline engines achieve only about 25-30% efficiency in practice, while the best diesel engines achieve 35-40%, and even the most efficient production cars rarely exceed about 40%. An 81% efficiency would mean that only 19% of the fuel's energy is lost to heat. The second law of thermodynamics limits a heat engine to the Carnot efficiency, $$ 1 - T_c/T_h $$; 80.8% would not violate that bound for every conceivable pair of reservoir temperatures, but it is far beyond what ordinary car engines can achieve at their operating temperatures. (Showing a true thermodynamic impossibility would require specifying those temperatures and comparing with the Carnot limit.)

The claim is inconsistent with physical reality. Several factors reveal the impossibility:

First, gaining 3.00 km (about 1.86 miles) of altitude while traveling 100 km represents an average grade of 3%, which combined with overcoming friction over such distance requires substantial energy. Second, achieving a final speed of 30.0 m/s (67 mph) adds kinetic energy. The total energy requirement of 96.9 MJ is close to what 1.0 gallon of gasoline can provide (120 MJ), but this would require an efficiency far beyond that of any real car engine.

In reality, such a trip would require approximately 3-4 gallons of gasoline for a typical car, or about 1.5-2 gallons for a highly efficient hybrid vehicle. The advertising claim is clearly false and misleading.

**Answer**

(a) The claimed efficiency is **80.8%**.

(b) This efficiency is **unreasonably high**—it exceeds the efficiency of any real gasoline vehicle by a factor of 2-3.

(c) The premises are **inconsistent**. The fuel consumption of 1.0 gallon is far too low for the described trip. Realistically, this trip would require **3-4 gallons of gasoline** for a conventional car.

of a single stair step. Also consider why the same person can descend stairs at

**Guidance for Constructing This Problem**

When constructing this problem, consider the following framework:

**Strategy**

Use the relationship between power, work, and time. The work done climbing stairs is primarily against gravity (W = mgh), while descending involves negative work. Compare energy requirements for ascending versus descending.

**Key Parameters to Define:**

1. **Person's mass**: Choose a realistic value (60-100 kg)
2. **Sustainable power output**: Choose an assumed sustainable long-term **mechanical** power output (e.g., about 100 W). Do not use a Table 2 entry directly as mechanical output.
3. **Stair dimensions**: Typical stair step height is 0.18-0.20 m
4. **Time period**: Consider "long-term" as several hours

**Calculations to Include:**

1. Calculate the rate of climbing (steps per minute or floors per minute) based on:
   <div class="equation">
   $$ P = \frac{mgh}{t} $$
   </div>

   where h is the total height climbed in time t

2. Determine how many steps can be climbed per minute given the person's sustainable power output

3. Compare ascending vs. descending:
   - **Ascending**: Muscles do positive work against gravity, consuming metabolic energy
   - **Descending**: Muscles do negative work (eccentric contraction), acting as brakes to control descent. This requires less metabolic energy even though similar forces are involved

**Discussion Points:**

- When climbing, chemical energy is converted to gravitational potential energy plus heat
- When descending, gravitational potential energy is converted primarily to heat through muscle friction and joint absorption—muscles work eccentrically to prevent falling
- Descending can be done faster and with less fatigue because it requires much less metabolic energy, even though the forces on muscles are similar
- The fundamental difference is that ascending requires continuous energy input to increase potential energy, while descending dissipates existing potential energy

**Solution**

**Example Answer:**

For a 70 kg person with an assumed sustainable mechanical power output of 100 W (about 500 W metabolic at 20% efficiency) climbing stairs with 0.20 m step height:
- Rate of vertical climb: P/(mg) = 100 W / (70 kg × 9.8 m/s²) = 0.146 m/s
- Steps per minute: (0.146 m/s × 60 s/min) / 0.20 m = 44 steps/min
- If we additionally assume a descent rate of 100 steps/min, this is about 2.3 times the calculated ascent rate of 44 steps/min. The descent rate is a separate illustrative assumption, not a result of the climbing-power equation; negative muscular work still has a metabolic cost.

With the assumed $$100\text{ W}$$ of sustained mechanical output, the $$70\kg$$ person can climb approximately 44 steps per minute on $$0.20\m$$ steps, while estimating a descent rate requires an additional model.

Consider humans generating electricity by pedaling a device similar to a

the things to consider are the power output that is reasonable using the legs,

When constructing this problem, consider the following framework:

**Strategy**

Calculate the total power output needed from a large power plant, then determine how many people pedaling continuously would be required. Account for human limitations including sustainable power output, rest requirements, and 24-hour operation.

**Key Parameters to Define:**

1. **Power plant capacity**: Choose a realistic power plant size (e.g., 1000 MW for a large coal or nuclear plant, or 500 MW for a medium-sized facility)
2. **Individual power output**: Choose an explicitly assumed **mechanical** output that a person can sustain for hours, such as 100 W. 4. **Efficiency**: Include generator efficiency (~90%) converting mechanical to electrical power

   </div>

2. Determine number of people needed:
   <div class="equation">
   $$ N = \frac{P_{\text{plant}}}{P_{\text{effective per person}}} $$
   </div>

3. Calculate shift requirements for 24/7 operation

4. Consider total workforce including rest periods

- Compare the number of people required to the population of cities or countries
- Calculate the logistics: food energy input needed to power the cyclists vs. electricity output
- Consider that humans are only ~25% efficient at converting food energy to mechanical work

- Address practical impossibilities: space requirements, cost, coordination
- Highlight why mechanical and fuel-based power generation is necessary for modern civilization

::: problem

**Integrated Concepts**

A 105-kg basketball player crouches down 0.400 m while waiting to jump. After
exerting a force on the floor through this 0.400 m, their feet leave the floor and
their center of gravity rises 0.950 m above its normal standing erect position.
(a) Using energy considerations, calculate the player's velocity when they leave the
floor.
(b) What average force did the player exert on the floor? (Do not neglect the force to
support their weight as well as that to accelerate them.)
(c) What was the player's average power output during the acceleration phase? (Assume the force, and hence the acceleration, is constant during the push-off.)

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Use energy conservation for part (a). For part (b), use work-energy theorem over the crouch distance. For part (c), calculate power from work and time.

**Sketch and coordinate system**

![A vertical +y axis points up, with y = 0 at the center of gravity’s normal standing height, where the feet leave the floor. Crouched: y = −0.400 m, at rest. Leaving the floor: y = 0, speed v unknown, directed along +y. Top of the jump: y = +0.950 m, v = 0. During the push-off the floor pushes up with F along +y and the weight w = mg points along −y.](../resources/sketch_07_09_p10.svg){: .sketch}

Take $$ +y $$ upward with $$ y = 0 $$ at the normal standing height, where the feet leave the floor. The push-off covers $$ \Delta y = +0.400\m $$ (from $$ -0.400\m $$ to 0), during which the floor's force $$ F $$ points along $$ +y $$ and the weight along $$ -y $$. Afterward the player rises to $$ y = +0.950\m $$, where $$ v = 0 $$.

**Solution**

**(a)** Using energy conservation (KE at takeoff converts to PE at max height):

<div class="equation">
$$ \frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = mgh $$
</div>

<div class="equation">
$$ v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.80\mss)(0.950\m)} = 4.32\ms $$
</div>

**(b)** The player's legs must do work to give the body kinetic energy plus work against gravity over the 0.400 m crouch:

Net work needed:
<div class="equation">
$$ W_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \frac{1}{2}(105\kg)(4.32\ms)^{2} = 980\J $$
</div>

The average force must support the player's weight plus provide the net acceleration force:

<div class="equation">
$$ F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{d} + mg = \frac{980\J}{0.400\m} + (105\kg)(9.80\mss) = 2450\N + 1029\N = 3.47 \times 10^{3}\N $$
</div>

**(c)** Power output during acceleration:

Time during crouch (for constant acceleration from rest, $$ v_{\text{avg}} = v/2 $$):

<div class="equation">
$$ t = \frac{d}{v_{\text{avg}}} = \frac{0.400\m}{2.16\ms} = 0.185\s $$
</div>

Total work done by legs (including against gravity):
<div class="equation">
$$ W_{\text{total}} = F_{\text{avg}} \cdot d = (3.47 \times 10^{3}\N)(0.400\m) = 1388\J $$
</div>

Average power:
<div class="equation">
$$ P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W_{\text{total}}}{t} = \frac{1388\J}{0.185\s} = 7.50\text{ kW} $$
</div>

**Discussion**

The takeoff velocity of 4.32 m/s (about 9.7 mph) is reasonable for a professional basketball player performing a vertical jump. This speed translates to rising 0.950 m (about 3.1 feet) above the standing position, which represents an excellent vertical jump—typical professional players achieve 24-36 inches of vertical leap.

The average force of 3470 N is particularly noteworthy—it's about 3.4 times the player's body weight. This demonstrates the tremendous forces that athletes' legs must generate during explosive movements. The force is distributed between both legs, so each leg experiences about 1735 N (390 lbs), which while substantial, is within the capability of well-trained athletes.

The average power output is about 7.50 kW (about 10 horsepower). Under the constant-force idealization, the instantaneous power $$ P = Fv $$ grows as the player speeds up, reaching $$ (3.47 \times 10^{3}\N)(4.32\ms) = 15.0 \text{ kW} $$ at takeoff. A real jump does not have a constant force, and determining its true peak power would require the actual force-time profile, which is not given. This enormous power output lasts only a fraction of a second, which is characteristic of explosive athletic movements; it is many times the mechanical power a person can sustain while cycling. Such high peak power outputs are possible because they draw on immediate energy stores (ATP and creatine phosphate) in the muscles, which are quickly depleted.

The efficiency of this movement is also worth noting—the player's muscles must generate significantly more than the 1388 J of mechanical work due to the ~25% efficiency of muscle contraction, meaning the actual metabolic energy expended is approximately 5500 J or about 1.3 food calories per jump.

**Answer**

(a) The player's takeoff velocity is **4.32 m/s** (approximately 9.7 mph).

(b) The average force exerted on the floor is **3470 N** (about 780 lbs or 3.4 times body weight).

(c) The player's average power output during the acceleration phase is **7.50 kW** (about 10 hp), achievable for brief explosive movements.
