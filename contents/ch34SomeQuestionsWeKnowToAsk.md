Throughout the text we have noted how essential it is to be curious and to ask
questions in order to first understand what is known, and then to go a little
farther. Some questions may go unanswered for centuries; others may not have
answers, but some bear delicious fruit. Part of discovery is knowing which
questions to ask. You have to know something before you can even phrase a decent
question. As you may have noticed, the mere act of asking a question can give
you the answer. The following questions are a sample of those physicists now
know to ask and are representative of the forefronts of physics. Although these
questions are important, they will be replaced by others if answers are found to
them. Indeed, some questions on earlier versions of this list, such as where
bursts of $$\gamma $$ rays come from and what powers quasars, have since been
largely answered. The fun continues.

### On the Largest Scale

1. _What are the geometry and future of the universe_? Measurements of the
   cosmic microwave background show that space is flat to within less than one
   percent. Whether the universe expands forever, and how quickly, depends on
   the nature of dark energy.
2. _What is dark energy_? About 70% of the energy density of the universe seems
   to be in a form that accelerates the expansion of the universe, yet we do not
   know what it is. The simplest explanation is a cosmological constant, an
   energy that belongs to empty space itself. However, recent surveys that map
   millions of galaxies hint that dark energy may be weakening over time. If
   confirmed, this would rule out the simplest explanation and point to new
   physics.
3. _Why do different ways of measuring the expansion rate disagree_? The Hubble
   constant $$H_0$$ measured from nearby supernovae and variable stars is about
   $$73\text{ km/s/Mpc}$$, while the value inferred from the cosmic microwave
   background, using our standard model of cosmology, is about
   $$67\text{ km/s/Mpc}$$. This "Hubble tension" has grown sharper as the
   measurements have improved. Either some measurements share an unrecognized
   error, or our model of the universe is missing something.
4. _What is dark matter_? Its gravitational effects on galaxies, galaxy
   clusters, and the cosmic microwave background show that it is definitely
   there, but we do not know what it is. Increasingly sensitive underground
   detectors have not yet found the weakly interacting massive particles that
   were long the leading candidates. Other possibilities, such as very light
   particles called axions, are now being searched for. The answer could reveal
   whole new realms of physics and confirm the disturbing possibility that most
   of the matter out there is a completely different form of matter.
5. _How did the first galaxies form so quickly_? The James Webb Space Telescope
   has found bright, surprisingly massive galaxies less than 300 million years
   after the Big Bang. Our models of how the small fluctuations seen in the
   cosmic microwave background grew into galaxies must now explain how stars
   formed so efficiently, so early.
6. _How did supermassive black holes grow so large so early_? We now know that
   quasars are powered by matter falling into supermassive black holes, and that
   our own galaxy has one with about four million times the mass of the Sun at
   its center. Some quasars, however, have black holes of more than a billion
   solar masses when the universe was less than a billion years old. Whether
   these grew from the remnants of the first stars or formed directly from
   collapsing gas clouds is not known. Gravitational wave detectors are also
   finding black holes in the poorly understood "intermediate" mass range
   between stellar and supermassive black holes.
7. _What produces fast radio bursts_? These are flashes of radio waves lasting
   only milliseconds that arrive from distant galaxies. Thousands have now been
   detected. At least one came from a magnetar, a neutron star with an
   extraordinarily strong magnetic field, in our own galaxy. But some bursts
   repeat and others do not, and it is not clear whether one kind of source can
   explain them all.
8. _Why is the universe made of matter rather than antimatter_? The Big Bang
   should have produced equal amounts of matter and antimatter, which would have
   annihilated into radiation. Instead, a small excess of matter survived to
   form everything we see. The laws of physics do treat matter and antimatter
   slightly differently (a property called CP violation), but the differences
   measured so far are far too small to explain the excess.

### On the Intermediate Scale

1. _How do phase transitions take place on the microscopic scale_? We know a lot
   about phase transitions, such as water freezing, but the details of how they
   occur molecule by molecule are not well understood. Similar questions about
   specific heat a century ago led to early quantum mechanics. A related puzzle
   is the nature of glass, which becomes rigid like a solid while its molecules
   remain disordered like those of a liquid. Phase transitions are also examples
   of complex adaptive systems that may yield insights into other
   self-organizing systems.
2. _Is there a way to deal with nonlinear phenomena that reveals underlying
   connections_? Nonlinear phenomena lack a direct or linear proportionality
   that makes analysis and understanding a little easier. There are implications
   for nonlinear optics and broader topics such as chaos.
3. _Can we understand turbulence_? The swirling, chaotic flow of water from a
   faucet or air behind a car is governed by equations known since the 1800s,
   yet we cannot predict its detailed behavior. Mathematicians have not even
   proven that the equations of fluid flow always have well-behaved solutions,
   and a prize of one million dollars awaits anyone who settles the question.
4. _How do high- $${T}_{\text{c}} $$ superconductors become resistanceless at
   such high temperatures_? Copper-oxide superconductors were discovered in
   1986, and we still lack an accepted explanation of how they work. Since then,
   hydrogen-rich compounds have been found to superconduct within about
   50 degrees of room temperature, but only when squeezed to pressures over a million times
   atmospheric pressure. Several claims of room-temperature superconductors at
   ordinary pressure have not survived independent testing. Understanding how
   these materials work may help make them more practical or may result in
   surprises as unexpected as the discovery of superconductivity itself.
5. _There are magnetic and electronic effects in materials we do not
   understand—how do they work_? Although beyond the scope of this text, there
   is a great deal to learn in condensed matter physics (the physics of solids
   and liquids). Recent examples include topological materials, which conduct
   electricity on their surfaces but not in their interiors, and quantum spin
   liquids, in which magnetic moments never settle into an ordered pattern even
   near absolute zero. We may find surprises analogous to lasing, the quantum
   Hall effect, and the quantization of magnetic flux. Complexity may play a
   role here, too.
6. _Where is the boundary between the quantum and classical worlds_? Quantum
   mechanics allows an object to be in a superposition of states, yet we never
   see a baseball in two places at once. Interference has been observed with
   molecules containing about 2000 atoms, and quantum computers rely on keeping
   many particles in delicate superpositions. How large an object can show
   quantum behavior, and what exactly happens during a measurement, remain open
   questions.

### On the Smallest Scale

1. _Are quarks and leptons fundamental, or do they have a substructure_? The
   Large Hadron Collider (LHC) has found no sign of substructure at the highest
   energies yet reached. Its high-luminosity upgrade and proposed future
   colliders will look more closely, but there will also be input from
   cosmology and other systematics.
2. _Why do leptons have integral charge while quarks have fractional charge_? If
   both are fundamental and analogous as thought, this question deserves an
   answer. It is obviously related to the previous question.
3. _Why are there three families of quarks and leptons_? First, does this imply
   some relationship? Second, why three and only three families? And why do
   their masses range over more than a factor of $$10^{11}$$, from the
   neutrinos to the top quark?
4. _Why does the Higgs boson have the mass it has_? The Higgs boson, discovered
   at the LHC in 2012, gives the other particles their masses. Quantum effects
   should push its own mass up enormously, toward the scale at which the forces
   might be unified, unless something cancels them with extraordinary
   precision. Many physicists expected new particles, such as those predicted by
   supersymmetry, to appear at the LHC and provide that cancellation. None have
   been found so far.
5. _Are all forces truly equal (unified) under certain circumstances_? They
   don’t have to be equal just because we want them to be. The answer may have
   to be indirectly obtained because of the extreme energy at which we think
   they are unified.
6. _How can gravity be described by quantum mechanics_? General relativity and
   quantum mechanics are each extraordinarily successful, yet they are
   incompatible at the Planck scale, near $$10^{-35}\text{ m}$$. A related
   puzzle is what happens to the information carried by matter that falls into
   a black hole: quantum mechanics says information cannot be destroyed, but
   Hawking radiation seems to carry none of it away.
7. _Are there other fundamental forces_? Claims of a fifth force have come and
   gone over the decades, as have hints from particle experiments, such as a
   mismatch between the measured and predicted magnetism of the muon, that
   faded as measurements and calculations improved. Proposed new forces may be
   either extremely weak or very short ranged, making them extraordinarily
   difficult to detect in the presence of stronger forces. The question
   remains; and if there are no other forces, we need to ask why only four and
   why these four.
8. _Why does the strong force treat matter and antimatter the same_? Nothing in
   the theory of the strong force forbids it from distinguishing matter from
   antimatter, yet experiments show that it does not, to better than one part
   in $$10^{10}$$. One proposed explanation predicts a new particle, the axion,
   which is also a candidate for dark matter.
9. _Is the proton stable_? We have discussed this in some detail, but the
   question is related to fundamental aspects of the unification of forces.
   Experiments have shown that the proton lifetime is more than $$10^{34}$$
   years, and larger detectors under construction will push further. We may
   never know from experiment that the proton is stable, only that it is very
   long lived.
10. _Are there magnetic monopoles_? Many particle theories call for very massive
    individual north- and south-pole particles—magnetic monopoles. If they
    exist, why are they so different in mass and elusiveness from electric
    charges, and if they do not exist, why not?
11. _What are the neutrino masses, and is the neutrino its own antiparticle_?
    Oscillations establish nonzero masses, but the exact values and their
    ordering remain important questions. Direct measurements show that the
    electron neutrino mass is less than $$0.5\text{ eV}$$, about a millionth of
    the electron mass. If the neutrino is its own antiparticle, a rare process
    called neutrinoless double beta decay should occur, and searches for it are
    under way. The answers affect both particle physics and cosmology.
12. _What are the systematic characteristics of high- $$Z $$ nuclei_? All
    elements through $$Z=118$$ have been discovered; elements 115 and 117 were
    named moscovium and tennessine in 2016. Attempts to make elements 119 and
    120 are under way. It has long been conjectured that there may be an island
    of relative stability for nuclei with about 114 to 126 protons and 184
    neutrons, and the study of the most recently discovered nuclei will
    contribute to our understanding of nuclear forces.

These lists of questions are not meant to be complete or consistently
important—you can no doubt add to it yourself. There are also important
questions in topics not broached in this text that are of current interest to
physicists. Hopefully, the point is clear that no matter how much we learn,
there always seems to be more to know. Although we are fortunate to have the
hard-won wisdom of those who preceded us, we can look forward to new
enlightenment, undoubtedly sprinkled with surprise.

### Section Summary

- On the largest scale, the questions which can be asked may be about the
  geometry of the universe, dark energy, the expansion rate, dark matter, the
  first galaxies and black holes, fast radio bursts, and why matter outnumbers
  antimatter.
- On the intermediate scale, we can query about phase transitions, nonlinear
  phenomena, turbulence, high- $${T}_{c} $$ superconductors, magnetic and
  electronic effects in materials, and the boundary between the quantum and
  classical worlds.
- On the smallest scale, questions may be about quarks and leptons, the Higgs
  boson, unification of forces, quantum gravity, possible new forces, stability
  of protons, magnetic monopoles, neutrinos, and superheavy nuclei.
