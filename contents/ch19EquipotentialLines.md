- Explain equipotential lines and equipotential surfaces.
- Describe the action of grounding an electrical appliance.
- Compare electric field and equipotential lines.

:::

We can represent electric potentials (voltages) pictorially, just as we drew
pictures to illustrate electric fields. Of course, the two are related.
Consider [[Figure 1]](#Figure1), which shows an isolated positive point charge
and its electric field lines. Electric field lines radiate out from a positive
charge and terminate on negative charges. While we use blue arrows to represent
the magnitude and direction of the electric field, we use green lines to
represent places where the electric potential is constant. These are called **equipotential lines** in two dimensions, or _equipotential surfaces_ in three
dimensions. The term _equipotential_ is also used as a noun, referring to an
equipotential line or surface. The potential for a point charge is the same
anywhere on an imaginary sphere of radius $$r $$ surrounding the charge. This is
true since the potential for a point charge is given by $$V=kQ/r $$ and, thus,
has the same value at any point that is a given distance $$r $$ from the charge.
An equipotential sphere is a circle in the two-dimensional view
of [[Figure 1]](#Figure1). Since the electric field lines point radially away
from the charge, they are perpendicular to the equipotential lines.

![A positive point charge Q at the center. Twelve straight field lines, in blue, point radially outward. Four green equipotential circles surround the charge, drawn for equal steps of potential, so they are closest together near the charge, where the field is strongest. The field lines cross every equipotential at right angles.](../resources/Figure_19_04_01.svg 'An isolated point charge ( Q ) with its electric field lines in blue and equipotential lines in green. The potential is the same along each equipotential line, meaning that no work is required to move a charge anywhere along one of those lines. Work is needed to move a charge from one equipotential line to another. Equipotential lines are perpendicular to electric field lines in every case. ')
{: #Figure1}

It is important to note that **equipotential lines** are always perpendicular to
electric field lines . No work is required to move a charge along an
equipotential, since $$\Delta V=0 $$ . Thus the work is

<div class="equation" >
 $$W= -\Delta \text{PE}= -q\Delta V=0. $$
</div>

Work is zero if force is perpendicular to motion. Force is in the same direction
as $$\vb{E} $$ , so that motion along an equipotential must be
perpendicular to $$\vb{E} $$ . More precisely, work is related to the
electric field by

<div class="equation" >
 $$W=Fd \cos \theta =qEd \cos \theta =0. $$
</div>

Note that in the above equation, $$E $$ and $$F $$ symbolize the magnitudes of
the electric field strength and force, respectively. Neither $$q $$ nor
$$\vb{E} $$ nor $$d $$ is zero, and so $$\cos \theta $$ must be 0,
meaning $$\theta $$ must be $$90 ^\circ $$ . In other words, motion along an
equipotential is perpendicular to $$\vb{E} $$.

One of the rules for static electric fields and conductors is that the electric
field must be perpendicular to the surface of any conductor. This implies that
a **conductor is an equipotential surface in static situations** . There can be
no voltage difference across the surface of a conductor, or charges will flow.
One of the uses of this fact is that a conductor can be fixed at zero volts by
connecting it to the earth with a good conductor—a process called **grounding**.
Grounding can be a useful safety tool. For example, grounding the metal case of
an electrical appliance ensures that it is at zero volts relative to the earth.

Because a conductor is an equipotential, it can replace any equipotential
surface. For example, in [[Figure 1]](#Figure1) a charged spherical conductor
can replace the point charge, and the electric field and potential surfaces
outside of it will be unchanged, confirming the contention that a spherical
charge distribution is equivalent to a point charge at its center.

[[Figure 2]](#Figure2) shows the electric field and equipotential lines for two
equal and opposite charges. Given the electric field lines, the equipotential
lines can be drawn simply by making them perpendicular to the electric field
lines. Conversely, given the equipotential lines, as in [[Figure 3]](#Figure3)(
a), the electric field lines can be drawn by making them perpendicular to the
equipotentials, as in [[Figure 3]](#Figure3)(b).

One of the most important cases is that of the familiar parallel conducting
plates shown in [[Figure 4]](#Figure4). Between the plates, the equipotentials
are evenly spaced and parallel. The same field could be maintained by placing
conducting plates at the equipotential lines at the potentials shown.

![Plate A on the left is positive at 100 V and plate B on the right is negative at 0 V. Blue field lines run straight across from A to B, evenly spaced, and bow outward at the ends. Green equipotential lines at 75 V, 50 V and 25 V are straight, evenly spaced, and parallel to the plates, crossing the field lines at right angles.](../resources/Figure_19_04_04.svg 'The electric field and equipotential lines between two metal plates.')
{: #Figure4}

An important application of electric fields and equipotential lines involves the
heart. The heart relies on electrical signals to maintain its rhythm. The
movement of electrical signals causes the chambers of the heart to contract and
relax. When a person has a heart attack, the movement of these electrical
signals may be disturbed. An artificial pacemaker and a defibrillator can be
used to initiate the rhythm of electrical signals. The equipotential lines
around the heart, the thoracic region, and the axis of the heart are useful ways
of monitoring the structure and functions of the heart. An electrocardiogram (
ECG) measures the small electric signals being generated during the activity of
the heart. More about the relationship between electric fields and the heart is
discussed in [Energy Stored in Capacitors](../contents/ch19EnergyStoredInCapacitors).

### Section Summary

- An equipotential line is a line along which the electric potential is
  constant.
- An equipotential surface is a three-dimensional version of equipotential
  lines.
- Equipotential lines are always perpendicular to electric field lines.
- The process by which a conductor can be fixed at zero volts by connecting it
  to the earth with a good conductor is called grounding.

### Conceptual Questions

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

What is an equipotential line? What is an equipotential surface?

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Define sets of points sharing a potential value.

**Solution**

An equipotential line is a curve along which electric potential is constant; an equipotential surface is a two-dimensional surface on which potential is constant. Moving a fixed charge between two points on either gives $$\Delta U=q\Delta V=0$$. For a single point charge, spherical surfaces centered on the charge are equipotentials, and their intersections with a plane through the charge are circles.

**Discussion**

An equipotential line or surface thus connects points with the same electric potential, so the electric field does no work along it.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Explain in your own words why equipotential lines and surfaces must be perpendicular to electric field lines.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Test whether the field could have any component tangent to an equipotential.

**Solution**

For a small tangential displacement, $$dV=-\vec E\cdot d\vec r=0$$. If the field had a tangential component, this dot product would be nonzero for some displacement along the equipotential, contradicting its constant potential. The nonzero field must therefore be normal to the equipotential.

**Discussion**

At a zero-field point the field has no direction, so the perpendicularity statement applies where the field is nonzero. Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines because there can be no electric force component doing work along an equipotential.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="conceptual-questions">

::: problem

Can different equipotential lines cross? Explain.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Ask whether one point can have two different potentials.

**Solution**

Equipotential lines representing different potential values cannot cross: their intersection would assign two potentials to the same point. Curves representing the same value are parts of the same level set; such a set can meet itself at a critical point where $$\vec E=0$$, as in a saddle-point contour.

**Discussion**

Thus different-valued equipotentials never cross, although branches of a single equipotential can meet at a zero-field point.

:::

</div>

### Problems &amp; Exercises

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

(a) Sketch the equipotential lines near a point charge $$+q $$ . Indicate the direction of increasing potential. (b) Do the same for a point charge $$-3 \text{q} $$.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Equipotential lines for a point charge are concentric circles (spheres in 3D) centered on the charge. The potential from a point charge is $$V = kQ/r$$, so all points at the same distance have the same potential.

**Solution**

**(a) For charge +q:**

The equipotential lines are concentric circles centered on the positive charge. Since $$V = kq/r$$:

- The potential is positive everywhere
- Potential **increases** as you move **toward** the charge (smaller $$r$$)
- The innermost circles have the highest potential
- Arrows indicating increasing potential point **inward** toward the charge

**(b) For charge -3q:**

The equipotential lines are also concentric circles, but:

- The potential is negative everywhere (since $$Q < 0$$)
- The magnitude of potential increases as you move toward the charge
- Potential **increases** (becomes less negative) as you move **away** from the charge
- Arrows indicating increasing potential point **outward** away from the charge
- The equipotential lines are more closely spaced near the charge (for the same voltage intervals) because the charge magnitude is 3 times larger

![Two panels drawn with the same potential step between neighbouring equipotentials. (a) A charge +q: the equipotentials are concentric circles at +1, +2, +3, and +4 units, crowding together toward the charge; the potential increases inward. (b) A charge −3q: the circles at every whole-number potential from −3 to −12 units are also concentric; with the same step, the circles are three times as far out for a given potential, so three times as many fit in a given region because the charge is three times larger, and the potential increases outward, away from the charge. Radial field lines point outward from +q and inward toward −3q.](../resources/concept_19_04_p01.svg)

**Discussion**

The key difference is the direction of increasing potential: toward a positive charge, away from a negative charge. This is consistent with the fact that positive charges create positive potentials and negative charges create negative potentials. The spacing of equipotential lines indicates the strength of the electric field—closer spacing means stronger field.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

Sketch the equipotential lines for the two equal positive charges shown in [[Figure 5]](#Figure5). Indicate the direction of increasing potential.

![Two equal positive charges +q side by side. Computed field lines leave each charge in all directions and curve away from the other charge; no line runs between them, and midway there is a point where the field is zero.](../resources/Figure_19_04_05.svg 'The electric field near two equal positive charges is directed away from each of the charges.')
{: #Figure5}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Equipotential lines must be perpendicular to electric field lines at every point. For two equal positive charges, we use the principle that the electric potential from multiple charges adds algebraically: $$V = V_1 + V_2$$. Near each charge, equipotentials are nearly circular; far away, they become circular (like a single charge of +2q); in between, they have more complex shapes determined by the requirement of perpendicularity to field lines.

**Solution**

The equipotential lines must be perpendicular to the electric field lines at every point.

**Description of the sketch:**

1. **Near each charge:** The equipotential lines are nearly circular, centered on each charge individually.

2. **In the region between the charges:** Let the charges be a distance $$2a$$ apart. At the midpoint, the fields cancel and the potential is $$V_0 = 2kq/a$$. The midpoint is a **saddle point** of the potential. Along the line joining the charges, $$V$$ is lowest at the midpoint and rises toward each charge. Along the perpendicular bisector, $$V$$ is _highest_ at the midpoint and falls off as $$V(0, y) = 2kq/\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}$$. The perpendicular bisector is therefore **not** an equipotential. The equipotentials are:
   - $$V > V_0$$: two separate closed loops, one around each charge;
   - $$V = V_0$$: a single figure-eight curve whose two lobes enclose the two charges and cross each other at the midpoint;
   - $$V < V_0$$: single closed curves enclosing both charges. Just below $$V_0$$ they are pinched at the waist (peanut-shaped); at lower potentials they become ovals and then circles.

3. **Far from both charges:** The equipotential lines become approximately circular, centered on the midpoint between the charges (the system looks like a single charge of +2q from far away).

4. **Direction of increasing potential:** Arrows point **toward** either charge. The highest potential regions are closest to either positive charge.

5. **Between the charges:** The potential is higher than at infinity but lower than very close to either charge. Every equipotential is a closed curve around one or both charges; only the figure-eight at $$V = V_0$$ passes through the midpoint.

![Computed equipotentials of two equal positive charges. Close to each charge they are small circles. The equipotential through the midpoint, drawn heavier, is a figure eight whose two lobes enclose the two charges and cross at the midpoint, where the field is zero (a saddle point of the potential). Lower potentials give single closed curves around both charges, pinched at the waist at first and becoming nearly circular farther out. The perpendicular bisector is not an equipotential: the potential is highest at the midpoint along it. Faint field lines run outward from both charges, crossing every equipotential at right angles. The potential increases toward either charge.](../resources/concept_19_04_p02.svg)

**Discussion**

The equipotential lines form closed curves that must either encircle one charge or both charges. Since both charges are positive, the potential is positive everywhere and increases as you approach either charge.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

[[Figure 6]](#Figure6) shows the electric field lines near two charges $${q}_{1} $$
and $${q}_{2} $$ ,
the first having a magnitude four times that of the second. Sketch the equipotential lines for these two charges, and indicate the direction of increasing potential.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

First, interpret the electric field lines in the figure to determine the signs and relative magnitudes of the charges. Field lines point away from positive charges and toward negative charges. Once we know the charge configuration, we sketch equipotentials perpendicular to the field lines, noting that potential increases toward positive charges and away from negative charges. The asymmetry in charge magnitudes will create asymmetric equipotential patterns.

**Solution**

From the figure, $$q_1$$ is negative (field lines point toward it) and $$q_2$$ is positive (field lines point away), with $$|q_1| = 4|q_2|$$.

**Description of the sketch:**

1. **Near $$q_1$$ (negative, larger):** Equipotential lines are nearly circular and closely spaced due to the larger charge magnitude. Potential is strongly negative near this charge.

2. **Near $$q_2$$ (positive, smaller):** Equipotential lines are nearly circular but more widely spaced. Potential is positive but smaller in magnitude.

3. **Between the charges:** No equipotential connects the two charges, because the potential goes to $$-\infty$$ at $$q_1$$ and to $$+\infty$$ at $$q_2$$, so no single finite value can reach both. (It is the _field lines_ that run from one charge to the other.) The $$V = 0$$ equipotential is the set of points where $$k|q_1|/r_1 = kq_2/r_2$$, that is, $$r_1 = 4r_2$$. This is a **circle around the smaller positive charge** (a sphere in three dimensions). If the charges are a distance $$d$$ apart, the circle crosses the line joining them at $$d/5$$ from $$q_2$$ on the side toward $$q_1$$, and at $$d/3$$ from $$q_2$$ on the far side. Its radius is $$4d/15$$. Inside this circle the equipotentials are positive closed loops around $$q_2$$. Outside it they are negative: small loops around $$q_1$$ close to it, and, farther out, closed curves that enclose both charges and the zero circle.

4. **Direction of increasing potential:** Arrows point **away from $$q_1$$** (the negative charge) and **toward $$q_2$$** (the positive charge).

![Computed equipotentials of a charge q1 = −4q on the left and q2 = +q a distance d to its right. The zero-potential equipotential, drawn heavier, is a circle around the smaller positive charge: it crosses the line between the charges one fifth of the way from q2 to q1, and crosses the axis again d/3 beyond q2; its radius is 4d/15. Inside it, positive equipotentials are small loops around q2. Outside it, the potential is negative: small loops surround q1 closely, and farther out closed curves enclose both charges and the zero circle. No equipotential connects the two charges. Faint field lines run from q2 to q1 and in toward q1 from far away. The potential increases toward q2 and away from q1.](../resources/concept_19_04_p03.svg)

**Discussion**

The asymmetry in charge magnitude creates an asymmetric pattern. The equipotential lines are more densely packed around the larger negative charge, indicating a stronger field there.

:::

</div>

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

Sketch the equipotential lines a long distance from the charges shown in [[Figure 6]](#Figure6). Indicate the direction of increasing potential.

![Two charges, q1 on the left and q2 on the right. Computed field lines point away from q2 and toward q1. Four times as many lines end on q1 as leave q2: every line leaving q2 curves over to end on q1, and the rest come in to q1 from far away.](../resources/Figure_19_04_06.svg 'The electric field near two charges.')
{: #Figure6}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

At large distances from any charge configuration, the system appears as a single point charge with net charge $$Q_{\text{net}} = q_1 + q_2$$. The equipotential lines become spherical surfaces (circles in 2D) centered on the charge system. We use the far-field approximation $$V \approx kQ_{\text{net}}/r$$, which produces circular equipotentials. Because $$V$$ depends on $$1/r$$, equipotentials drawn at equal voltage intervals are _not_ evenly spaced in radius.

**Solution**

At large distances, any system of charges appears as a single point charge with the net charge $$Q_{net} = q_1 + q_2 = -4q + q = -3q$$.

**Description of the sketch:**

1. **Far from both charges:** The equipotential lines become nearly **circular** (spherical in 3D), centered approximately on the centroid of the charge distribution.

2. **Since the net charge is negative:** The potential is negative everywhere at large distances and approaches zero at infinity.

3. **Direction of increasing potential:** Arrows point **outward** (away from the charge system), since potential increases (becomes less negative) as distance increases.

4. **Spacing:** For equal voltage intervals, the circles are **not** evenly spaced. From $$V = kQ_{\text{net}}/r$$, the radial gap between neighbouring equipotentials is approximately $$\Delta r \approx |\Delta V|\, r^2/(k|Q_{\text{net}}|)$$, so the gaps grow as $$r^2$$ and the circles get farther apart with distance. For example, the $$-30$$ V, $$-20$$ V, and $$-10$$ V circles lie at radii in the ratio $$1 : 1.5 : 3$$.

![Seen from far away, the charges −4q and +q, drawn as a tiny pair at the centre, act like a single charge −3q. Computed equipotentials at equal potential steps, −1, −2, −3, and −4 units, are circles centred on the pair at radii in the ratio 1 : 1/2 : 1/3 : 1/4, so the gaps between neighbouring circles grow with distance (roughly as r squared). The potential is negative and increases outward, toward zero at infinity.](../resources/concept_19_04_p04.svg)

**Discussion**

This is an example of the far-field approximation: any charge distribution looks like a point charge from sufficiently far away. The net charge determines the far-field behavior.

:::

::: problem

Sketch the equipotential lines in the vicinity of two opposite charges, where the negative charge is three times as great in magnitude as the positive. Indicate the direction of increasing potential.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

For opposite charges with $$q_- = -3q$$ and $$q_+ = +q$$, we recognize this as a dipole-like configuration. The key is finding the zero-potential surface where $$V = k(q_+/r_+ - 3q/r_-) = 0$$, which occurs where $$r_+ = r_-/3$$. Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines, with potential increasing toward the positive charge and away from the negative charge. The net charge is $$-2q$$, affecting far-field behavior.

**Solution**

With $$q_{-} = -3q$$ and $$q_{+} = +q$$, the net charge is $$-2q$$.

**Description of the sketch:**

1. **Near the positive charge (+q):** Equipotential lines are nearly circular with positive potential values. Lines are more widely spaced (smaller charge, weaker field).

2. **Near the negative charge (-3q):** Equipotential lines are nearly circular with negative potential values. Lines are more closely spaced (larger charge, stronger field).

3. **Between the charges:** There is a point where $$V = 0$$, located closer to the smaller positive charge (at about 1/4 of the distance from the positive charge, since the ratio of distances must equal the ratio of charge magnitudes for $$V = 0$$).

4. **Direction of increasing potential:** Arrows point **toward the positive charge** and **away from the negative charge**.

5. **Far away:** Equipotential lines become circular, representing the net charge of $$-2q$$.

![Computed equipotentials of a charge −3q on the left and +q a distance d to its right. The zero equipotential, drawn heavier, is a circle around the positive charge: it crosses the line between the charges a quarter of the way from +q to −3q, and crosses the axis again d/2 beyond +q; its radius is 3d/8. Inside it, positive equipotentials are loops around +q. Outside it, the potential is negative: loops surround −3q closely, and farther out closed curves enclose both charges, becoming circles like those of a single charge −2q. Faint field lines run from +q to −3q and in toward −3q from far away. The potential increases toward the positive charge and away from the negative charge.](../resources/concept_19_04_p05.svg)

**Discussion**

The zero-potential surface forms a closed curve encircling the positive charge, with all points inside having positive potential and all points outside having negative potential (except very close to the positive charge).

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

Sketch the equipotential lines in the vicinity of the negatively charged conductor in [[Figure 7]](#Figure7). How will these equipotentials look a long distance from the object?

![The figure shows a negatively charged conductor that is shaped like an oblong.](/resources/Figure_19_04_07.svg 'A negatively charged conductor.')
{: #Figure7}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium are equipotential surfaces—all points on and inside have the same potential. The first equipotential outside the conductor follows its shape closely. Electric field lines are perpendicular to the conductor surface, so equipotentials are also perpendicular to field lines. At large distances, the conductor appears as a point charge, making equipotentials circular.

**Solution**

**Near the conductor:**

1. The conductor surface itself is an **equipotential surface** (all conductors are equipotentials in electrostatic equilibrium).

2. The first equipotential line outside the conductor follows the shape of the conductor closely—it has the same oblong shape.

3. The charge density is higher at the more curved ends of the oblong, so the electric field is stronger there and equipotential lines are more closely spaced near the ends.

4. **Direction of increasing potential:** Since the charge is negative, potential increases as you move **away** from the conductor. Arrows point outward.

**At large distances:**

The equipotential lines become **circular** (spherical surfaces in 3D), centered on the object. From far away, any finite charge distribution appears as a point charge.

![Computed equipotentials around a negatively charged, rounded oblong conductor. The conductor itself is one equipotential. The nearest equipotentials follow its oblong shape and are most crowded off the rounded ends, where the negative surface charge, shown by minus signs, is most dense and the field is strongest. Farther out they become rounder, and far away they are circles centred on the object, like those of a point charge. Faint field lines point inward, meeting the conductor at right angles. The potential increases outward, away from the conductor.](../resources/concept_19_04_p06.svg)

**Discussion**

The transition from conductor-shaped equipotentials near the surface to circular equipotentials far away is gradual. This is a general principle: local geometry matters near an object, but far away, only the total charge matters.

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

Sketch the equipotential lines surrounding the two conducting plates shown in [[Figure 8]](#Figure8), given the top plate is positive and the bottom plate has an equal amount of negative charge. Be certain to indicate the distribution of charge on the plates. Is the field strongest where the plates are closest? Why should it be?

![Two flat conducting plates seen edge on. The top plate tilts upward to the right; the bottom plate is nearly horizontal. They are closest together at the left end and farthest apart at the right. The top plate is positive and the bottom plate has an equal amount of negative charge.](../resources/Figure_19_04_08.svg ' ')
{: #Figure8}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Each plate is a conductor, so each is an equipotential, and charge concentrates on the facing surfaces because opposite charges attract. These plates are not parallel. The same potential difference is spread across a gap that widens from left to right, so the equipotentials between the plates fan out: they are crowded where the gap is small and spread apart where it is large. Since $$E \approx \Delta V/d$$ locally, the field is strongest where the plates are closest.

**Solution**

**Charge distribution on plates:**

The charge concentrates on the **facing surfaces** of the plates (the surfaces closest to each other). Additionally, charge density is higher at the **edges** of the plates, particularly at the corners where the curvature is greatest.

**Equipotential lines:**

1. **Between the plates:** The equipotentials fan out from the narrow end like the ribs of a partly open fan. Each one runs between the plates roughly parallel to them, and they are evenly divided across the gap at any one place. They are crowded where the plates are closest and spread apart toward the open end. Higher potential (more positive) is near the top plate.

2. **Near the edges:** Equipotential lines curve outward, following the fringe fields.

3. **Outside the plates:** Positive equipotentials wrap around the top plate and negative ones around the bottom plate, with the $$V = 0$$ equipotential running between them. The plates carry equal and opposite charges, so far away the pattern is that of a dipole, not of a single charge. Positive loops lie on the top side, negative loops on the bottom side, and the equipotentials do not become circles around the pair.

**Is the field strongest where the plates are closest?**

**Yes.** The field is strongest where the plates are closest because:

1. **Same voltage difference over smaller distance:** locally $$E \approx \Delta V/d$$, so smaller $$d$$ means larger $$E$$.

2. **Closer equipotential lines:** Where plates are closer, the same potential difference is spread over less distance, so equipotential lines are more closely spaced—indicating stronger field.

3. **Charge concentration:** Opposite charges attract, so charges migrate toward the facing surfaces, increasing the surface charge density and field there.

![Computed equipotentials around two conducting plates that are closest at the left and spread apart toward the right; the top plate is positive and the bottom plate negative. Between the plates the equipotentials fan out from the narrow left end like the ribs of a partly open fan: they are crowded together where the plates are closest, so the field is strongest there, and spread apart toward the open right end. Around the outer edges they curve around the plates. Positive charge, shown by plus signs, sits mainly on the lower face of the top plate, and negative charge on the upper face of the bottom plate, most densely at the narrow end and at the corners. The potential increases from the bottom plate toward the top plate.](../resources/concept_19_04_p07.svg)

**Discussion**

These tilted plates form a capacitor whose field is not uniform: the widening gap makes the field strongest at the narrow end and weaker toward the open end, in addition to the fringe fields at the edges. The parallel-plate result $$E = \Delta V/d$$ applies only locally, over a region small enough that the gap is nearly constant. When the plates are made parallel, the equipotentials between them become evenly spaced and the field becomes uniform away from the edges.

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

(a) Sketch the electric field lines in the vicinity of the charged insulator in [[Figure 9]](#Figure9). Note its non-uniform charge distribution. (b) Sketch equipotential lines surrounding the insulator. Indicate the direction of increasing potential.

![A diagonal insulating rod marked with plus signs. Most of the positive charge is crowded near its upper-right end, a few charges are in the middle, and one is at the lower-left end.](../resources/Figure_19_04_09.svg 'A charged insulating rod such as might be used in a classroom demonstration.')
{: #Figure9}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

Unlike conductors, insulators can maintain non-uniform charge distributions. Field line density reflects local charge density—more field lines where charge is more concentrated. Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines everywhere. Since all charges are positive, potential increases toward the rod, with the highest values near the most heavily charged region. The non-uniform charge creates non-uniform field strength and equipotential spacing.

**Solution**

**(a) Electric field lines:**

The electric field lines radiate outward from the positive charges, with density proportional to charge density:

- **Near the heavily charged end:** Many field lines radiate outward in all directions, closely spaced.
- **Near the middle:** Fewer field lines, more widely spaced.
- **Near the lightly charged end:** Very few field lines.
- Field lines never cross. They are **not** required to leave the rod perpendicular to its surface. That rule holds for a conductor in equilibrium, whose surface is an equipotential, and an insulator's surface is not an equipotential. Lines leaving the lightly charged middle and far end tilt away from the heavily charged end, because its charge pushes on them.

**(b) Equipotential lines:**

1. **Near the heavily charged end:** Equipotential lines are closely spaced and curve around this end, nearly circular close to the surface.

2. **Along the rod:** Equipotential lines are more widely spaced where charge density is lower.

3. **Direction of increasing potential:** Arrows point **toward** the rod (toward the positive charges). The highest potential is at the heavily charged end.

4. **Far from the rod:** Equipotential lines become approximately circular, centered on the center of charge of the distribution.

![Two computed panels for a diagonal insulating rod whose positive charges are crowded near its upper-right end, with a few in the middle and one at the lower-left end. (a) Field lines start on the charges and run outward; they are dense near the heavily charged end, and lines from the middle and lower parts tilt away from that end instead of leaving at right angles. (b) Equipotentials: small, closely spaced loops wrap the heavily charged end; the lower contours cross the rod, because an insulator surface is not an equipotential; farther out the contours become larger, rounder closed curves around the whole rod. The potential increases toward the rod and is highest at the heavily charged end. Equipotentials and field lines cross at right angles.](../resources/concept_19_04_p08.svg)

**Discussion**

Unlike conductors, insulators can maintain non-uniform charge distributions. The field and potential pattern reflects this non-uniformity. Because the rod's surface is not an equipotential, equipotential lines cross the rod (the heavily charged end is at a higher potential than the far end), and field lines meet its surface at an angle. The one rule that always holds is that field lines are perpendicular to equipotential lines everywhere.

:::

::: problem

The naturally occurring charge on the ground on a fine day out in the open country is $$-1.00 {\text{nC/m}}^{2} $$ .
(a) What is the electric field relative to ground at a height of 3.00 m? (b) Calculate the electric potential at this height. (c) Sketch electric field and equipotential lines for this scenario.

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

The electric field near an infinite plane of charge is given by $$E = \sigma/(2\epsilon_{0})$$. However, for the ground (which is a conductor with an image charge effect), the field is $$E = \sigma/\epsilon_{0}$$. The potential is found using $$V = Ed$$ for a uniform field.

**Solution**

**(a) Electric field at 3.00 m height:**

For a conductor (ground), the electric field just above the surface is:

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_{0}} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C/m}^2}{8.85 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2/(\text{N·m}^2)}$$

$$E = 113 \text{ V/m}$$

The field points downward (toward the negatively charged ground).

**(b) Electric potential at 3.00 m:**

Taking ground as $$V = 0$$:

$$V = Ed = (113 \text{ V/m})(3.00 \text{ m}) = 339 \text{ V}$$

The potential is positive at height because we moved upward against the downward-pointing field (away from the negative charges).

**(c) Sketch description:**

- **Electric field lines:** Vertical lines pointing downward (toward the ground), uniformly spaced.
- **Equipotential lines:** Horizontal lines parallel to the ground, with equal spacing for equal voltage intervals. Potential increases with height.
- Ground level is 0 V; at 3 m height, it's about 340 V.

![The ground carries negative charge, shown by minus signs along its surface. The electric field lines are vertical, evenly spaced, and point down toward the ground; the field is 113 V/m. The equipotentials are horizontal lines parallel to the ground, equally spaced for equal potential steps: 0 V at the ground, 113 V at 1 m, 226 V at 2 m, and 339 V at 3 m. The potential increases with height.](../resources/concept_19_04_p09.svg)

**Discussion**

This fair-weather electric field of about 100 V/m is typical. It's caused by charge separation in the atmosphere and is maintained by thunderstorm activity globally. A person 2 m tall standing on the ground has a potential difference of about 200 V between their head and feet! However, the tiny currents involved (the air is a very poor conductor) make this harmless.

**(a) The electric field is 113 V/m, directed downward.**

**(b) The electric potential at 3.00 m is 339 V.**

**(c) The sketch shows vertical field lines pointing down and horizontal equipotential lines with potential increasing upward.**

:::

<div class="exercise" data-element-type="problems-exercises">

::: problem

The lesser electric ray (**Narcine bancroftii**) maintains an incredible charge on its head and a charge equal in magnitude but opposite in sign on its tail ([[Figure 10]](#Figure10)). (a) Sketch the equipotential lines surrounding the ray. (b) Sketch the equipotentials when the ray is near a ship with a conducting surface. (c) How could this charge distribution be of use to the ray?

![The figure shows a photo of a Narcine bancroftii, an electric ray that maintains a strong charge on its head and a charge equal in magnitude but opposite in sign on its tail.](../resources/Figure_19_04_10.jpg "Lesser electric ray (Narcine bancroftii) (credit: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, NOAA's Fisheries Collection).")
{: #Figure10}

:::

::: solution

**Strategy**

The ray's charge configuration forms an electric dipole with equal and opposite charges separated by a distance. For part (a), we sketch dipole equipotentials: nearly circular loops near each charge, a zero-potential surface between them, and, far away, the dipole pattern $$V \approx kp\cos\theta/r^2$$. For part (b), a conducting surface is itself an equipotential; charges induced on it distort the nearby pattern so that field lines meet it at right angles. For part (c), we consider biological uses of electric fields: stunning prey, defense, electrolocation, and communication.

**Solution**

**(a) Equipotential lines surrounding the ray:**

The ray acts like an electric dipole with positive charge on its head and negative charge on its tail.

- **Near the head (positive):** Equipotential lines are closely spaced, concentric, and positive in value.
- **Near the tail (negative):** Equipotential lines are closely spaced, concentric, and negative in value.
- **Between head and tail:** There is a $$V = 0$$ equipotential line passing through the middle region, forming a plane perpendicular to the axis of the ray.
- **Far from the ray:** No equipotential encloses both charges, because the $$V = 0$$ plane separates the positive potentials on the head side from the negative potentials on the tail side. Far away, the potential is approximately $$V \approx kp\cos\theta/r^2$$, where $$p$$ is the dipole moment and $$\theta$$ is measured from the tail-to-head axis. An equipotential $$V$$ = constant is the curve $$r^2 \propto \cos\theta$$: a closed loop on one side of the zero plane that bulges out along the axis and pinches in toward the ray's middle. Larger loops correspond to smaller $$|V|$$. Unlike the far field of a net charge, these loops never become circles centered on the ray, and the potential falls off as $$1/r^2$$ instead of $$1/r$$.
- This pattern resembles that of a classic electric dipole.

**(b) Equipotentials near a conducting ship:**

When the ray approaches a conducting surface:

- The ship's conducting hull is an **equipotential**: in electrostatic equilibrium the whole surface is at one potential. Its value is a boundary condition, not something conductivity sets by itself. If the hull is effectively grounded through the surrounding seawater, we can take it as $$V = 0$$.
- **Field lines** meet the conductor perpendicularly. The **equipotential lines** near the hull run parallel to its surface, hugging its shape, because the surface is itself one of them.
- Charges induced on the hull (negative near the ray's head, positive near its tail) distort the dipole pattern: equipotentials between the ray and the hull are squeezed and flattened to follow the hull, and the field there is strengthened. The image-charge method models this by replacing the hull with a mirror-image dipole.

**(c) How the charge distribution is useful:**

The electric ray uses this charge distribution for several purposes:

1. **Stunning prey:** The voltage between head and tail can reach 30-50 volts. When the ray contacts prey, current flows through the prey, stunning or killing it.

2. **Defense:** The electric shock can deter predators.

3. **Navigation/sensing:** Some electric fish use their electric field to sense their environment (electrolocation), detecting distortions in the field caused by nearby objects.

4. **Communication:** Electric signals may be used for communication with other electric rays.

![Two computed panels treating the electric ray as a dipole, positive head on the right and negative tail on the left. (a) In open water: nearly circular equipotentials surround each end; the zero-potential equipotential is the straight perpendicular bisector between head and tail; positive loops lie on the head side and negative loops on the tail side, and no equipotential encloses both ends. Larger loops have smaller potentials and are pinched toward the middle, never becoming circles around the ray. (b) Above a grounded conducting hull along the bottom: the hull is itself the zero equipotential, and the equipotentials between the ray and the hull are flattened and squeezed to run along it, while those above the ray look much as in (a). The potential increases toward the head and away from the tail.](../resources/concept_19_04_p10.svg)

**Discussion**

Electric rays and other electric fish have specialized electric organs made of modified muscle cells (electrocytes) stacked in series to produce voltage. The biological capacitor-like arrangement allows them to store charge and discharge it rapidly when needed.
