# Exercises and solutions review: chapters 1–10

Review date: 2026-10-19. Source baseline: commit `78fbfb3f` (clean working tree at the start of this review). This is a review report; textbook sources have not been edited.

## Method and scope

Reviewed in table-of-contents order, chapter by chapter: **790 exercises across 73 files**, comprising **541 end-of-section problems and 249 other exercises**. Each statement and supplied solution was read for agreement between the request, reasoning, and answer, including equations, arithmetic, units, signs, assumptions, significant figures, and relevant figures. Suspect calculations were recomputed and diagrams underlying identified geometry errors were inspected directly. This is a manual technical review with supporting checks, not a formal proof of every calculation or an exhaustive rendering/accessibility audit. Conceptual questions and check-your-understanding exercises are included. Open-ended problems need a sample construction rather than a unique numerical answer.

The accompanying [coverage CSV](ch01-10-exercise-coverage.csv) lists every exercise in order, with its source line, local identifier, solution availability, and report references. Of the 541 problems, **7 have no sample solution and 12 provide numerical answers without working**. Among the 249 other exercises, **207 have no supplied solution**; these are recorded as availability gaps, since many conceptual questions intentionally have no printed answer.

Problem identifiers below are **local to each file**: P1 is its first end-of-section problem; C1 is its first other exercise. Source links point to the exercise opening, so the statement and solution can be read together. P1/P2 indicate priority only when written in brackets: [P1] materially incorrect or contradictory physics; [P2] a wrong numerical answer, missing requested reasoning, or an assumption/data mismatch; [P3] a smaller editorial or presentation issue.

Automated baselines: `python3 find_incomplete_solutions.py --range 1-10` reports 541 problems and 39 completeness flags; `python3 check_math.py --range 1-10` scans 73 files, runs 69 existing worked-example checks, and flags three numeric comparisons. Neither check establishes that a solution's physics or diagram interpretation is correct. In particular, missing heading labels are not necessarily missing reasoning.

## Chapter 1

Coverage: 55 exercises (36 problems, 19 other exercises).

- **[P2] The orbital-speed solution introduces a different radius.** [Physical quantities, P10](../contents/ch1PhysicalQuantitiesAndUnits.md#L866) asks students to obtain the Earth–Sun distance from Table 3, which supplies only `10^11 m`. That input gives `2π(10^11)/(3.16×10^7) = 1.99×10^4 m/s`, about 20 km/s. The solution instead introduces `1.5×10^11 m` and returns 30 km/s. Supply the more precise radius in the question/table, or accept the estimate supported by the stated source.
- **[P3] Significant-figure claims conflict with ambiguous input notation.** [Physical quantities, P1](../contents/ch1PhysicalQuantitiesAndUnits.md#L495) and [P4](../contents/ch1PhysicalQuantitiesAndUnits.md#L610) say that `100 km/h` and `100 yd` each justify three significant figures. Chapter 1 itself correctly explains that trailing zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous. Write `1.00×10^2` when three figures are intended, or describe the road speed as an estimate and the field length as a defined dimension.
- **[P3] The soccer-width conversion rounds before the last step.** [Physical quantities, P5](../contents/ch1PhysicalQuantitiesAndUnits.md#L648) writes `280×12 = 3.3×10^3`; the displayed product is 3360, which rounds to `3.4×10^3` at two figures. The final `3.3×10^3 in` is correct if computed directly from `85×3.281×12 = 3346.62`. Retain guard digits in the intermediate width.
- **[P3] State the speedometer bounds before rounding.** [Accuracy, P3](../contents/ch1AccuracyPrecisionAndSignificantFigures.md#L516) obtains `90±4.5 km/h` but then writes `85.5≈85` and `94.5≈95`. The exact computed interval is 85.5–94.5 km/h, or 53.1297–58.7223 mph. If 85–95 is intended as an outward-rounded enclosure, label that convention; it is not ordinary rounding of both endpoints.
- **[P3] A discussion overgeneralizes an instrument model.** [Accuracy, P9](../contents/ch1AccuracyPrecisionAndSignificantFigures.md#L799) says percent uncertainty stays constant for a given instrument. This is an assumption explicitly supplied for this problem, not a general property of instruments. Say “under the constant-percent-uncertainty assumption in this question.”
- **[P3] Clarify the time-uncertainty request.** [Accuracy, P14](../contents/ch1AccuracyPrecisionAndSignificantFigures.md#L1024), part (b), asks for “the uncertainty in the elapsed time” after already giving 1 s; the solution computes percent uncertainty. Add “percent” to part (b).
- **[P3] Remove an unsupported surface-smoothness comparison.** [Approximation, P6](../contents/ch1Approximation.md#L391) concludes that a scaled Earth would be smoother than a regulation billiard ball. A ratio of relief to diameter does not establish this without a ball surface-roughness specification; delete the comparison or provide a compatible quantitative criterion. The requested order-of-magnitude ratios are correct.

The automated flag for [Physical quantities, P3](../contents/ch1PhysicalQuantitiesAndUnits.md#L588) is a heading-format issue: the conversion is actually worked out. The `70/3≈25 years` comparison flagged by the arithmetic scanner is acceptable for the stated 70–80-year lifetime estimate and does not warrant a mathematical-error finding.

## Chapter 2

Coverage: 104 exercises (66 problems, 38 other exercises).

- **[P1] Half Dome solution confuses release sound with impact sound.** [Falling objects, P13](../contents/ch2FallingObjects.md#L1492) says sound travels upward, computes `t_hear=t_fall+t_sound`, and concludes that the tourist hears the sound after impact and cannot use it as a warning. The question concerns the sound of the rock **breaking loose at the top**, which travels downward and arrives after `250/335=0.746 s`. Impact occurs at `sqrt(500/9.8)=7.1429 s`; the time remaining after reaction is `7.1429−0.7463−0.300=6.0966 s`, or 6.10 s. The final answer is correct, but much of the strategy, intermediate timeline, and discussion contradict it. Rewrite those parts around a single timeline.
- **[P2] Premature subtraction rounding changes both train-passage times.** [Constant acceleration, P17](../contents/ch2MotionEquationsForConstantAccelerationInOneDimension.md#L2098) rounds `sqrt(421)` and `sqrt(382)` before subtracting from 22. The correct times are `(22−sqrt(421))/0.150=9.878 s` and `(22−sqrt(382))/0.150=16.368 s`, hence **9.88 s** and **16.4 s**, rather than 10.0 s and 16.7 s. The speeds 20.5 and 19.5 m/s are correct. Recompute the additional clearance interval and discussion from unrounded values; the second calculation in the discussion is evidence of the error, not an independent confirmation of 16.7 s.
- **[P2] Swan takeoff time is misrounded.** [Constant acceleration, P12](../contents/ch2MotionEquationsForConstantAccelerationInOneDimension.md#L1686) gives 17.0 s; `6.00/0.350=17.142857 s`, which is **17.1 s**. The distance 51.4 m is correct. This solution also supplies only answers, with no working.
- **[P2] Jet-car acceleration uses points absent from the graph.** [Graphical analysis, P1(b)](../contents/ch2GraphicalAnalysisOfOneDimensionalMotion.md#L474) claims Figure 15 passes through `(0,0)` and `(20,100)`. The image shows approximately `(0,15)` and `(20,115)`. Its slope still is 5.0 m/s², but the worked graph reading is wrong. Use the actual intercept and plotted values.
- **[P2] Claimed tangent in the acceleration graph does not pass through the curve.** [Graphical analysis, P4](../contents/ch2GraphicalAnalysisOfOneDimensionalMotion.md#L628) uses tangent points `(5,100)` and `(20,148)`. That line gives 116 m/s at 10 s, while Figure 17 gives about 210 m/s there. A tangent of slope 3.2 through `(10,210)` would instead pass near `(5,194)` and `(20,242)`. Draw or specify a tangent that actually touches the plotted curve; do not choose coordinates solely to reproduce the target slope.
- **[P2] Jogger tangent coordinates are inconsistent with the position plot.** [Graphical analysis, P6(b)](../contents/ch2GraphicalAnalysisOfOneDimensionalMotion.md#L748) uses `(5,40)` and `(10,20)` as tangent points near 7.5 s. - **[P2] Subway solution answers a different request.** [Graphical analysis, P5](../contents/ch2GraphicalAnalysisOfOneDimensionalMotion.md#L678) asks for velocity and acceleration histories, but the strategy and answer describe constructing the already supplied position graph. Explicitly give `v=2.0 m/s, a=0` for `0<t<10 s` and `v=2.0+1.2(t−10) m/s, a=1.2 m/s²` for `10<t<20 s`, with continuous velocity at 10 s. Its numerical derivation is otherwise consistent with the assumptions stated in the question.
- **[P2] Maximum compression is underdetermined without a force/time model.** [Falling objects, P16(d)](../contents/ch2FallingObjects.md#L1800) and [P18(d)](../contents/ch2FallingObjects.md#L2075) ask for ball compression from the incoming/outgoing speeds and total contact time. These determine average acceleration, not the stopping displacement. The solutions explicitly introduce constant acceleration until maximum compression; put this extra modeling assumption in the questions and label the results as estimates. A rigid floor alone does not supply it.
- **[P3] Runner solution labels displacement as distance.** [Constant acceleration, P6](../contents/ch2MotionEquationsForConstantAccelerationInOneDimension.md#L1179) calls 20.0 m the distance for a constant-acceleration motion that reverses after 4.50 s. In that mathematical model 20.0 m is displacement and total distance is 20.5 m. If the intended physical model is braking to rest and remaining there, the distance is 20.25 m and final speed zero. The solution discusses the latter interpretation but should distinguish these quantities explicitly and avoid saying reversal is impossible in general.
- **[P3] Motorcycle unit equivalence is wrong.** [Constant acceleration, P9](../contents/ch2MotionEquationsForConstantAccelerationInOneDimension.md#L1432) equates 26.8 m/s with 100 km/h. It is 96.48 km/h (approximately 60 mph). The numerical solution consistently uses 26.8 m/s; correct the parenthetical and its repetition in the discussion.

## Chapter 3

Coverage: 91 exercises (70 problems, 21 other exercises).

- **[P1] The minimum-speed formula for clearing a fence is incorrect.** [Projectile motion, P27](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L3017), discussion point 3, supplies an incorrect denominator and says 45° gives the minimum speed despite the fence being above release height. For horizontal distance `x` and height increase `Δy`, use `v_min = sqrt(g(Δy+sqrt(x²+Δy²)))` and `tan θ_opt=(Δy+sqrt(x²+Δy²))/x`. For its sample `x=20 m, Δy=1 m`, these are **14.35 m/s** and **46.43°**. Its two angles for a chosen 15 m/s are otherwise reasonable. Also replace “higher, shorter trajectory” with a precise distinction: the higher arc has a longer flight time to the same fence.
- **[P2] Hikers' components use the wrong diagram angle.** [Graphical vectors, P3](../contents/ch3VectorAdditionAndSubtractionGraphicalMethods.md#L620) reads Figure 18 as 50° north of east; the image shows **40°**. The components are **3.21 km north and 3.83 km east**, not the reverse. The figure's alternative text correctly says 40°.
- **[P2] Quarterback solution uses an obsolete, inconsistent ground speed.** [Addition of velocities, P5](../contents/ch3AdditionOfVelocities.md#L880) claims the earlier projectile problem yielded 16.3 m/s and derives 18.2 m/s at 22.3°. That earlier problem correctly gives `sqrt(18×9.8/sin50°)=15.1748 m/s`. Adding 2.00 m/s to its horizontal component gives **17.0084 m/s at 22.1515°**, or **17.0 m/s at 22.2°** with ordinary rounding. Rewrite the working and discussion; the existing final 17.0 m/s is close to correct, and the disagreement is not rounding of the displayed derivation.
- **[P2] Headwind vector equation has the wrong sign.** [Addition of velocities, P1](../contents/ch3AdditionOfVelocities.md#L567) writes `v_air = v_ground + v_wind`, while defining the wind vector opposite to the aircraft's motion. The correct relation is `v_plane/air = v_plane/Earth − v_air/Earth`. Its numerical answer 5.53 m/s is correct because it adds the positive **headwind magnitude**, but the vector statement and accompanying explanation are wrong.
- **[P2] A faster current cannot be canceled by this swimmer.** [Addition of velocities, P14](../contents/ch3AdditionOfVelocities.md#L1745) suggests aiming upstream to reach the point directly opposite. The swimmer's speed through water is only 0.5 m/s, less than the 0.8 m/s current. No heading cancels the downstream component. Keep the calculated crossing answer, but explain that reaching directly opposite would require a faster swimmer or another change in conditions.
- **[P2] Tennis discussion reverses the angle tradeoff.** [Projectile motion, P13](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L1690) says a steeper downward angle makes net clearance easier and landing in the box harder. At fixed speed and release height, it lowers the trajectory at the net and shortens the landing distance: clearance becomes harder, and overshooting the far service line becomes less likely. The calculated 6.1° and 5.3 m are reasonable.
- **[P2] Dunk timing explanation reverses the near-basket case.** [Projectile motion, P22](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L2586) says a player jumping too close reaches maximum height before reaching the rim. At fixed horizontal speed, jumping too close means reaching the rim **before** maximum height. Jumping too far means reaching it after maximum height. The numerical launch distance 1.96 m is correct.
- **[P2] “Percent of light speed” is wrong by roughly 270.** [Addition of velocities, P17](../contents/ch3AdditionOfVelocities.md#L2003) calls 26.6 km/s “2.4% of the speed of light.” It is **0.00887% of c**. Retain the critique of constant gravity, but remove this erroneous comparison.
- **[P3] Retain precision through nearly canceling vector sums.** [Graphical vectors, P5–P6](../contents/ch3VectorAdditionAndSubtractionGraphicalMethods.md#L735) give a direction 4.7° using rounded components; unrounded inputs give **4.6484°**, or 4.65° at three figures. [P7](../contents/ch3VectorAdditionAndSubtractionGraphicalMethods.md#L878) writes `26.7≈26.6 m`; 26.6115 m is obtained directly from unrounded components, not by rounding 26.7. [Analytical vectors, P11](../contents/ch3VectorAdditionAndSubtractionAnalyticalMethods.md#L1254) similarly jumps from 7.36/63.3 to 7.34/63.5; the final **7.3433 km, 63.4708°** is correct, but the working should actually produce it.
- **[P3] Curvature answer rounds incorrectly.** [Projectile motion, P9(c)](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L1343) calculates 80.4 m and then calls 80.0 m the three-significant-figure result. Keep **80.4 m**. For the requested drop below the ship's horizontal tangent, the exact geometry is `(R_E−h)²+d²=R_E²`, rather than `(R_E+h)²=R_E²+d²`; both yield the same leading approximation `d²/(2R_E)` at this scale.
- **[P3] Wind-gust working does not produce the final answer.** [Projectile motion, P23](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L2680) writes `56.9≈57.4 m`. The final 57.4 m is correct: use `R'=60.0−1.50 sqrt(30.0/9.80)=57.3755 m`. Preserve the unrounded rise time, about 1.7496 s, in the component calculation.
- **[P3] Use “speed” when asking for maxima and minima.** [Projectile motion, C1(b)](../contents/ch3ProjectileMotion.md#L654) asks when “velocity” is minimum/maximum. Vectors have no scalar ordering; ask about speed or velocity magnitude.
- **[P3] Separate wind relative to water from wind relative to a boat.** [Addition of velocities, P11](../contents/ch3AdditionOfVelocities.md#L1456) correctly calculates wind/water but then equates it with the wind felt on a moving sailboat. The latter additionally requires subtracting the boat's velocity through the water.

## Chapter 4

Coverage: 82 exercises (54 problems, 28 other exercises).

- **[P1] Spring oscillation does not eliminate spacecraft recoil.** [Newton's second law, P4](../contents/ch4NewtonsSecondLawOfMotion.md#L706) correctly identifies the relative-acceleration error, but then says a spring chair avoids recoil entirely because there is no net force over a cycle. The spacecraft still experiences an instantaneous reaction force, and its motion affects the relative oscillation. An isolated two-mass model has `ω²=k(1/m+1/M)`, not `k/m`; large spacecraft mass makes the correction small, rather than zero. Propose balanced simultaneous forces on two opposed loads, or measure/correct spacecraft acceleration. Merely attaching the device to a massive part of the same spacecraft does not remove the reaction.
- **[P1] The contact force vanishes after the jumper leaves the floor.** [Further applications, P7](../contents/ch4FurtherApplicationsOfNewtonsLawsOfMotion.md#L926) says “The force decreases to just the player's weight once he leaves the floor.” The normal/contact force becomes **zero**; weight remains downward, so net acceleration is `−g`. The numerical push-off answer is correct.
- **[P2] Include the ground support when the lunar module cannot lift off on Earth.** [Newton's second law, P14](../contents/ch4NewtonsSecondLawOfMotion.md#L1349) computes `F_net=−68000 N` and says this negative net force means the module remains on the ground. At rest, the ground supplies **68000 N upward**, giving **zero net force**. Label `T−mg` as the unsupported force sum and include the normal force for the grounded case, as the later rocket problem in Problem-Solving Strategies already does.
- **[P2] Do not generalize the resisting-force contradiction to all friction.** [Problem-solving strategies, P16](../contents/ch4ProblemSolvingStrategies.md#L1206) says friction “can only reduce acceleration, never increase it” and “cannot help accelerate the vehicle forward.” Static tire–road friction is precisely what accelerates the car. The negative value is impossible for the **assumed backward resisting-force magnitude** in this model; it is not a prohibition on forward friction or negative signed force components.
- **[P2] Momentarily motionless does not establish equilibrium.** [Further applications, P3](../contents/ch4FurtherApplicationsOfNewtonsLawsOfMotion.md#L654) sets net force to zero solely because the rescued person is “momentarily motionless.” Zero instantaneous velocity does not imply zero acceleration. Change the premise to “held stationary” or explicitly specify equilibrium. Under that premise the tensions are correct (about 736 N and 193.6 N).
- **[P2] Requested free-body diagrams are incomplete or absent.** [Newton's third law, P2(c)](../contents/ch4NewtonsThirdLawOfMotion.md#L394) never supplies the requested sketches/diagrams for the two rugby players. [Problem-solving strategies, P2](../contents/ch4ProblemSolvingStrategies.md#L210) and [P6(c)](../contents/ch4ProblemSolvingStrategies.md#L471) list only horizontal forces even though full free-body diagrams are requested; include weight and support. [P12(a)](../contents/ch4ProblemSolvingStrategies.md#L947) describes the people and combined system but omits the separately requested rope diagram. [P8(b,c)](../contents/ch4ProblemSolvingStrategies.md#L663) explains the vector constructions but does not draw the requested scale drawings.
- **[P3] Correct the tension-arrow directions.** [Normal/tension, P3](../contents/ch4NormalTensionAndOtherExamplesOfForces.md#L714) and [P5](../contents/ch4NormalTensionAndOtherExamplesOfForces.md#L857) describe tension forces on the central load as pointing **below** horizontal, while their equations correctly use upward components. On the central load the two forces point outward **above** horizontal; below-horizontal arrows would not support it.
- **[P3] Clarify the external forces in tug of war.** [Normal/tension, P1](../contents/ch4NormalTensionAndOtherExamplesOfForces.md#L599) must mean each person's horizontal force **on the ground**, not force on the rope, to justify the combined-system calculation. Specify this in the question. Rope pulls are internal to the teams-plus-rope system, and unequal total endpoint tensions are incompatible with an ideal massless rope.
- **[P3] Dolphin-force calculation rounds too early.** [Further applications, P4](../contents/ch4FurtherApplicationsOfNewtonsLawsOfMotion.md#L744) should give `35.0×4.50/2.30 = 68.478…`, or **68.5 N**, rather than 68.6 N from prematurely rounding acceleration.

- **[P1] Dog-sled acceleration incorrectly subtracts maximum static friction during motion.** [Friction, P6](../contents/ch5Friction.md#L737) uses `μ_s N` to obtain a nonzero “starting from rest” acceleration of 3.29 m/s² and coupling force 980 N. Before slipping, static friction adjusts and the sled has zero acceleration; once the applied drive exceeds the threshold and the runners slide, use kinetic friction. The acceleration immediately after motion begins is **3.52 m/s²**, and coupling force is about **945 N**, as in part (b). Reframe part (a) as a threshold question if a distinct static-friction calculation is intended. The following ice-skater problem correctly makes this distinction, so the two solutions currently teach inconsistent rules.

- **[P2] Several bending exercises lack a solvable introductory-level numerical target.** [Elasticity, P6](../contents/ch5Elasticity.md#L1000), [P12](../contents/ch5Elasticity.md#L1276), and [P16(b)](../contents/ch5Elasticity.md#L1480) ask how far poles bend, while their own solutions correctly explain that the linear bending model fails for the supplied loads/stiffnesses. A depth of about 11 km gives `ρgh≈1.1×10^8 Pa`, around 1100 atm; `2.0×10^8 Pa` is roughly **twice**, not 200 times, that pressure. The requested bulk-modulus estimate itself is correct.
- **[P2] Bottle-pressure rounding and unit conversions disagree.** [Elasticity, P13](../contents/ch5Elasticity.md#L1340) says `4×10² N/cm²` has two significant figures (it has one) and writes `3.6×10^6 Pa=40 bar` (it is **36 bar**, approximately 36 atm). - **[P3] Four-wheel drive doubles traction, not uphill acceleration.** [Friction, P14–P15](../contents/ch5Friction.md#L1339) describe the accelerations as approximately halved/doubled. The common downhill gravity term does not scale with the number of drive wheels: compare **4.20→9.09**, **2.74→6.16**, and **−0.195→+0.294 m/s²**. - **[P3] Correct the climber force directions and alternative text.** [Friction, P17](../contents/ch5Friction.md#L1606) has a 15° angle to the horizontal in the image, while its alternative text says to the rock face. On the climber, tension points upward/toward the cliff and the combined foot-contact reaction points upward/away. Keep action and reaction on separate bodies. The numerical results are consistent with the image.
- **[P3] Raindrop comparison needs a stated sound speed.** [Drag, P6](../contents/ch5DragForces.md#L709) calls 313 m/s faster than sound without specifying temperature; this is below the roughly 340 m/s air sound speed used elsewhere in the book. - **[P3] Low speed alone does not verify Stokes' regime.** [Drag, P9](../contents/ch5DragForces.md#L867) says steady descent “confirms” laminar flow and Stokes' law. Also state the neglect of buoyancy (or provide oil density) in the question, since the solution shows a roughly 12% change when including it.

## Chapter 6

Coverage: 73 exercises (50 problems, 23 other exercises).

- **[P1] Earth–Moon comparison attributes a real spatial difference to rounding.** [Gravitation, P5](../contents/ch6NewtonsUniversalLawOfGravitation.md#L711) compares lunar gravity at the **barycenter** with the acceleration of the **Earth's center**, then says they must be equal and the 2.4% difference is rounding. They are evaluated at different distances from the Moon. The Earth's center accelerates according to lunar gravity at the Earth's center; the stronger lunar field at the barycenter need not equal that acceleration. Keep the two numerical results and explain the inverse-square gradient, rather than claiming exact equality at different points.
- **[P1] Swing loop argument neglects the tension constraint.** [Centripetal acceleration, P13](../contents/ch6CentripetalAcceleration.md#L915) says having enough energy to reach the top means the child completes the circular loop. For a flexible suspension, maintaining tension requires `v_top²≥gr`, hence `v_bottom²≥5gr`. With `r=2.00 m`, the minimum bottom speed is **9.90 m/s**, above the given 9.00 m/s. The suspension goes slack **before** the top. Retain the 40.5 m/s² and 905 N bottom results; replace the claimed complete loop with this constraint.
- **[P2] Earth's lifetime-travel comparisons use incorrect conversions.** [Centripetal acceleration, P3](../contents/ch6CentripetalAcceleration.md#L328) correctly estimates `4×10^21 m`, but calls this “400 trillion kilometers” and “25 million light-years.” It is approximately **4×10^18 km** and **4.2×10^5 light-years**. Correct or remove both comparisons.
